## Review flags and resolutions
- L12-RF1 (p. 155): lead iodide is called "not soluble". It is only very slightly soluble (about
  0.076 g per 100 cm³ at 20 °C). → Game says it "barely dissolves" everywhere, and Q02 states the
  limitation. Round 1 removed the last "won't dissolve", "refuses to dissolve" and "stays
  undissolved" wording from Q04 and Q08. (PubChem, lead iodide solubility.)
- L12-RF2 (pp. 156–157): cooling nitrogen dioxide is told as a simple one-way change. NO₂ and N₂O₄
  are in equilibrium in a closed tube, cooling shifts the mixture toward colorless N₂O₄, and
  leftover NO₂ leaves a faint yellow tint. That matches the graded tubes in the p. 157 figure.
  → Q16–Q18 and the O3 card say cooling favors the colorless gas and that both gases are present.
  The order item (Q17) uses graded colors; since the second review cycle it orders the tubes by
  the amount of colorless dinitrogen tetroxide. Learners are not asked for the word "equilibrium".
  Both gases are toxic, so no procedure appears. (Rutgers CLDF "Temperature Effect on the
  Equilibrium Between Two Gases".)
- L12-RF3 (p. 158): the photo caption says the sugar is "burned". The body text says it is heated
  until it breaks apart into caramel, and also calls caramel "crystals". → Game follows the body
  text: sugar is heated until it browns and smells of caramel. It never says the sugar burns or
  calls caramel crystals. (Textbook only.)
- L12-RF4 (p. 159): the spread concludes "a new substance" from one lower melting point. Impurities
  also lower and broaden a melting range. The solubility test gives no temperature, and the
  textbook values differ somewhat from the reference values (widened in round 1):

  | Property | Salicylic acid | Aspirin |
  |---|---|---|
  | Melting point | about 158–159 °C | about 135 °C |
  | Water solubility | 2.24 g/L at 25 °C | reported from about 2.5 g/L (15 °C) to 4.6 g/L (25 °C) |
  | Density | about 1.44 g/cm³ | listed 1.35–1.40 g/cm³ |

  A packed powder contains air gaps, so its measured density would normally read below the true
  density; the textbook's 1.4 g/cm³ readings are treated as idealized example data.
  → Questions present the numbers as "the textbook's example" and state that both solubility tests
  were done at the same temperature. Since the second review cycle, Q19 scales the textbook's
  per-liter solubility figures to 2 L, and Q20 treats the lower melting point as one of two changed
  results. Q23, the O4 card and the melting point glossary entry teach that a pure substance melts
  within a narrow range (about a degree or two), that impurities lower and broaden the range, and
  that agreeing tests make a stronger claim. Q21 uses the reference densities. No question asks for
  an exact melting point, solubility or density of aspirin. (LibreTexts, Nichols, "6.1B: Uses of
  Melting Points", whose table lists pure benzoic acid at 120–122 °C; LibreTexts, Sandtorv, "5.3:
  Melting Point Analysis - Identity and Purity"; PubChem aspirin and salicylic acid records.)
- L12-RF5 (pp. 154, 159, 161): the textbook says products have "entirely different" properties,
  but its own p. 159 example matches the reactant in color and density. → Game says products differ
  in at least some characteristic properties. Q09 repairs the misconception using the first two
  p. 159 results, the O2 card watchOut names it, and the melting point glossary entry notes that two
  substances can share a melting point. Round 1 corrected the OpenStax supports text (the page does
  not say color and density can be shared) and added LibreTexts 6.1B, which says two different
  compounds can coincidentally have similar or identical melting points. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e
  1.3; LibreTexts 6.1B.)
- L12-RF6 (p. 155): the printed sentence defining chemical and physical properties is missing words,
  and its wording on flammability is awkward. → The glossary, key idea 4, the O2 card and Q10 use
  OpenStax-based definitions. Since round 1 a chemical property is described as whether and how a
  substance can change, because OpenStax includes "the inability to change"; Q11 depends on that.
  Since the second review cycle, Q11 and the O2 card say the products "show no sign of reacting"
  with vinegar rather than that they "don't react at all". (OpenStax Chemistry 2e 1.3.)
- L12-RF7 (pp. 160–161): two history claims are inaccurate.
  - p. 160 says "1826, a French chemist" isolated salicin. NLM dates the French pharmacist Henri
    Leroux's isolation to 1829.
  - p. 161 says Hoffmann "devised and patented" his method in 1897. He made pure acetylsalicylic
    acid at Bayer in 1897, but the German patent was rejected and the U.S. patent ran from 1900.
    Eichengrün's role is debated.

  - Sources disagree on the patent (added in the second review cycle): NLM says Bayer patented
    aspirin in 1899, while the Science History Institute says the German application was rejected
    and the U.S. patent ran 1900–1917.

  → Game says "early 1800s" and never says who patented aspirin or when. No question tests dates,
  nationalities or credit, and Q27 orders the steps by lineage only. Since the second review cycle,
  Q27's limitation and the O5 card say the chain is the order of discovery: Kolbe synthesized
  salicylic acid in 1859 and Heyden sold synthetic salicylic acid from 1874, so later aspirin did
  not have to come from willow bark. (NLM "The Story of Aspirin"; Science History Institute, Felix
  Hoffmann.)
- L12-RF8 (pp. 160–161): the 1800s patient tests are called "clinical trials", and aspirin is
  described with "no reports of dizziness or breathing problems". Read alone, that suggests aspirin
  has no side effects. Round 1 adds two points:
  - Aspirin is itself converted to salicylic acid in the body (PubChem Metabolism/Metabolites).
  - Aspirin can trigger breathing problems in some people (MedlinePlus).

  So the textbook line is a historical report, not a lasting difference between the two medicines.
  The drawback of salicylic acid that historical accounts stress is stomach irritation (Science
  History Institute).
  → Game says aspirin met the criteria better, not perfectly (Q26, O5 card watchOut), and notes that
  modern trials are more carefully planned. Q26 now says aspirin can trigger breathing problems in
  some people. Q28 no longer uses the salicin-to-salicylic-acid finding as a reason to reject
  salicin. Since the second review cycle, the O5 card names stomach irritation first among
  salicylic acid's side effects and attributes dizziness and breathing problems to the textbook.
  No dosing or medicine advice appears anywhere, including the new Q29. (MedlinePlus "Aspirin" and "Clinical
  Trials"; PubChem aspirin Metabolism/Metabolites; Science History Institute.)
  - The science reviewer proposed saying the difference was "not a chemical difference". The
    reviser wrote "not a lasting difference between the two medicines" instead, because aspirin
    and salicylic acid are different substances, and aspirin is more, not less, associated with
    breathing reactions in sensitive people.
- L12-RF9 (pp. 154–158): the lesson involves lead compounds, toxic NO₂, matches and kitchen
  reactions. → These appear only as described scenarios or thought experiments (Q13), with no
  procedures. Round 1 rewrote the hook so it is no longer phrased as an instruction ("Pour…").
- L12-RF10 (pp. 154, 158): the lesson's color and gas clues could be read as proof. → The seed
  question Q05, plus Q06 and Q07, show that bubbles and temperature changes also happen when nothing
  new forms. Since round 1 the O1 card's watchOut and key idea 3 name boiling, a soda's fizz and the
  cooling from melting ice, so what Q06 and Q07 test is now also taught. Q03 calls melting and
  dissolving clues that are not proof. (LibreTexts 3.6, Changes in Matter.)
- L12-RF11 (p. 153, added in the second review cycle): the NGSS box (PS1.B) says the atoms of the
  original substances are regrouped into different "molecules". Several products in this lesson,
  such as potassium nitrate and sodium acetate, are ionic compounds made of ions, not separate
  molecules (the brief's guardrail: salt is an extended structure). → Key idea 1 now says the atoms
  are rearranged into "new combinations" and that no atoms are created or destroyed; no game text
  calls these products molecules. The O3 card's alt text counts the atoms on both sides of
  2 NO₂ → N₂O₄, whose gases really are molecular. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e 2.6 and 4.1.)
