## Objectives
- L12-O1 (observe): name reactants and products, and spot the property changes that hint a new
  substance formed. Families F01, F02, F04, F06 (6 questions).
- L12-O2 (relate): explain that a new substance has its own set of physical and chemical
  properties, and tell a reaction apart from a physical change. Families F03, F04, F05, F06, F08

- L12-O3 (relate): explain how heating, cooling or mixing starts a reaction, and why it depends on
  both the substances and the conditions. Families F07, F08 (5 questions).

  separate observation from conclusion, and judge the strength of the evidence. Families F03,
  F04, F09, F10, F11 (10 questions).
- L12-O5 (apply): evaluate competing medicine designs against criteria, using the salicylic acid
  and aspirin results. Families F11, F12 (4 questions).

## Review flags and resolutions
- L12-RF1 (p. 155): lead iodide is called "not soluble". It is only very slightly soluble (about
  0.076 g per 100 cm³ at 20 °C). → Game says it "barely dissolves", and Q02 states the limitation.
  (PubChem, lead iodide solubility.)
- L12-RF2 (pp. 156–157): cooling nitrogen dioxide is told as a simple one-way change. NO₂ and N₂O₄
  are in equilibrium in a closed tube, cooling shifts the mixture toward colorless N₂O₄, and
  leftover NO₂ leaves a faint yellow tint. That matches the graded tubes in the p. 157 figure.
  → Q16–Q18 and the O3 card say cooling favors the colorless gas and that both gases are present.
  The order item uses graded colors. Learners are not asked for the word "equilibrium". Both gases
  are toxic, so no procedure appears. (Rutgers CLDF "Temperature Effect on the Equilibrium Between
  Two Gases".)
- L12-RF3 (p. 158): the photo caption says the sugar is "burned". The body text says it is heated
  until it breaks apart into caramel, and also calls caramel "crystals". → Game follows the body
  text: sugar is heated until it browns and smells of caramel. It never says the sugar burns or
  calls caramel crystals. (Textbook only.)
- L12-RF4 (p. 159): the spread concludes "a new substance" from one lower melting point. Impurities
  also lower and broaden a melting range. The solubility test gives no temperature, and the
  textbook values differ slightly from the reference values:

  | Property | Salicylic acid | Aspirin |
  |---|---|---|
  | Melting point | about 158–159 °C | about 135 °C |
  | Water solubility at 25 °C | 2.24 g/L | about 3.3 g/L |
  | Density | 1.44 g/cm³ | 1.40 g/cm³ |

  → Questions present the numbers as "the textbook's example" and state that both solubility tests
  were done at the same temperature. Q23 and the O4 card teach that agreeing tests make a stronger
  claim, and Q21 uses the reference densities. No question asks for aspirin's exact melting point.
  (LibreTexts, Nichols, "6.1B: Uses of Melting Points"; PubChem aspirin and salicylic acid
  records.)
- L12-RF5 (pp. 154, 159, 161): the textbook says products have "entirely different" properties,
  but its own p. 159 example matches the reactant in color and density. → Game says products differ
  in at least some characteristic properties. Q09 repairs the misconception, and the O2 card
  watchOut names it. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e 1.3.)
- L12-RF6 (p. 155): the printed sentence defining chemical and physical properties is missing words,
  and its wording on flammability is awkward. → The glossary, O2 card and Q10 use OpenStax-based
  definitions. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e 1.3.)
- L12-RF7 (pp. 160–161): two history claims are inaccurate.
  - p. 160 says "1826, a French chemist" isolated salicin. NLM dates the French pharmacist Henri
    Leroux's isolation to 1829.
  - p. 161 says Hoffmann "devised and patented" his method in 1897. He made pure acetylsalicylic
    acid at Bayer in 1897, but the German patent was rejected and the U.S. patent ran from 1900.
    Eichengrün's role is debated.

  → Game says "early 1800s" and never says who patented aspirin or when. No question tests dates,
  nationalities or credit, and Q27 orders the substances by lineage only. (NLM "The Story of
  Aspirin"; Science History Institute, Felix Hoffmann.)
- L12-RF8 (pp. 160–161): the 1800s patient tests are called "clinical trials", and aspirin is
  described with "no reports of dizziness or breathing problems". Read alone, that suggests aspirin
  has no side effects. → Game says aspirin met the criteria better, not perfectly (Q26, O5 card
  watchOut), and notes that modern trials are more carefully planned. No dosing or medicine advice
  appears anywhere. (MedlinePlus "Aspirin" and "Clinical Trials".)
- L12-RF9 (pp. 154–158): the lesson involves lead compounds, toxic NO₂, matches and kitchen
  reactions. → These appear only as described scenarios or thought experiments (Q13), with no
  procedures.
- L12-RF10 (pp. 154, 158): the lesson's color and gas clues could be read as proof. → The seed
  question Q05, plus Q06 and Q07, show that bubbles and temperature changes also happen in physical
  changes. Q03, the O1 card and key idea 3 call these clues, not proof. (LibreTexts 3.6, Changes in
  Matter.)
