## Review flags and resolutions
- **L09-RF1** (p. 111)
  - Issue: the text says the Burgess Shale has yielded "over 60,000 different species". That is the scale of specimen counts, not species.
  - Resolution: no species count is used. Q15 tests why the site matters, which is that it preserves soft-bodied animals.
  - Sources: Royal Ontario Museum (about 200 species, over 150,000 specimens); UCMP "The Cambrian Period".
- **L09-RF2** (pp. 105, 112)
  - Issue: the end-Permian toll appears as 95 percent of lifeforms on p. 105 and 90 percent of species on p. 112. Outside sources also differ.
  - Resolution (updated round 1): the O3 card now says the end-Permian event was the worst and that most species died out, especially in the oceans; the earlier resolution described wording the card did not contain. No question keys a percentage, and the O3 watch-out names the disagreement.
  - Sources: NASA "The Great Dying" (about 9 in 10 marine and 7 in 10 land species); NHM London (more than 95 percent).
- **L09-RF3** (pp. 107, 116–117)
  - Issue: "only about 10 percent" for the Phanerozoic conflicts with the textbook's own numbers: 541 ÷ 4,600 ≈ 12 percent. The 541 Ma date is also out of date (538.8 Ma on ICS v2024/12).
  - Resolution: Q05 keys 12 percent (tolerance 0.5), and its limitations note both the textbook's rounding and the 538.8 Ma date. Elsewhere 541 Ma is kept as the lesson's value.
  - Sources: NPS "Geologic Time Scale" and "The Precambrian" (Precambrian about 88 percent); ICS chart.
- **L09-RF4** (p. 107)
  - Issue: "Most of the rock that makes up Earth's crust formed during the current eon" is questionable. The cores of the continents (cratons) are Precambrian. The Phanerozoic gets more chart space mainly because of its fossil record.
  - Resolution: Q06 keys the fossil-record reason, and the crust claim is not used.
  - Sources: USGS Youth and Education "Introduction"; NPS "The Precambrian"; USGS "Age of the Earth".
- **L09-RF5** (p. 110)
  - Issue: the text says cyanobacteria were "the first organisms to carry out photosynthesis" and equates stromatolites with cyanobacteria colonies. Other microbes photosynthesized earlier without releasing oxygen, stromatolites are built by microbial mats in general, and the 3.5 Ga start of oxygen release is debated.
  - Resolution: content says "first organisms known to release oxygen by photosynthesis". Q12 tests only the order of steps. Round 1: Q13 had stated the textbook's "well over a billion years" delay as fact; it now says "probably hundreds of millions of years" and its limitations record the debate. Cycle 2: Q13's limitations now say the textbook states (p. 111) a delay of more than a billion years, rather than that its timeline implies one. No question keys a start date.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 22.1; UCMP "Cyanobacteria: Fossil Record"; round 1 added Planavsky et al. 2014 (CaltechAUTHORS record) and MIT News 2021.
- **L09-RF6** (pp. 111, 116)

  - Resolution: content describes early relatives of most major animal groups appearing long after life began. Q14 repairs the "life began then" idea, and no question keys a duration.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 27.4 and 22.1; ICS chart.
- **L09-RF7** (pp. 112–113)

  - Resolution: content says "non-bird dinosaurs" for the 66 Ma extinction, and no question keys the 164-million-year figure for dominance. Round 1: Q19's table gives first dinosaurs about 230 Ma and the end of the non-bird dinosaurs at 66 Ma; its statement f uses that span only for how long they existed.

  - Issue: four dated or questionable details that content does not use:

    - oxygen reaching today's level, with the first land plants, at 400 Ma, though land plants go back to the Ordovician and oxygen later rose as high as about 35 percent;

    - (e, added in cycle 2) the p. 115 timeline (14 Ma, rendered) says apes in southern European grasslands evolved into modern apes and humans, though the oldest known members of the human family lived in Africa (Sahelanthropus, Chad, about 7 to 6 Ma).

No content uses (e); Q19 keeps only the textbook's 3.5 Ma upright-walking ancestors and names Sahelanthropus in its limitations.
  - Sources: UCMP "Introduction to the Cetacea"; NHM "When whales walked on four legs"; OpenStax Biology 2e 25.1; Smithsonian "The Age of Oxygen"; USGS "Paleozoic"; cycle 2 added Smithsonian Human Origins "Sahelanthropus tchadensis".
- **L09-RF17** (pp. 109–110; added round 1)
  - Issue: the textbook says a large part of Earth's mantle was blown into orbit to form the moon, but current models put debris from both Earth and the impactor into orbit. Separately, the p. 110 timeline lists methane and nitrogen among volcanic gases, and the original O2 card called methane a main volcanic gas, although volcanic gas is overwhelmingly water vapor, carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide.
  - Resolution: the glossary, the O2 card and Q11 say debris from both bodies, much of it like Earth's mantle rock, formed the moon. The O2 card says volcanoes released mostly water vapor and carbon dioxide, and that the early air also held nitrogen and some methane.
  - Sources: NASA Science "Moon Formation"; USGS FAQ "What gases are emitted by Kīlauea and other active volcanoes?".
- **L09-RF18** (p. 114; added in cycle 2)
  - Issue: the p. 114 timeline (34 Ma, rendered) gives one cause for the Antarctic ice cap: Antarctica separated from Australia, ocean circulation changed and temperatures fell. Scientists still debate the cause. Declining atmospheric CO₂ is considered the primary driver. The opening and deepening of the Southern Ocean seaways (Tasmanian Gateway and Drake Passage) reorganized currents and cooled Antarctic surface waters.
  - Resolution: the O3 card (paragraph 4) names both causes and says many scientists think falling carbon dioxide mattered most. No question keys a cause.
  - Source: Sauermilch et al. 2021, "Gateway-driven weakening of ocean gyres leads to Southern Ocean cooling", Nature Communications (open-access copy in the NERC Open Research Archive; PDF text extracted locally).
