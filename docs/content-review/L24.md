## Objectives
- L24-O1 (observe): tell temperature (the average energy of motion), thermal energy (the total
  for all particles) and heat (energy moving from warmer to cooler) apart. Families F01, F02, F03,
  F06, F11 (11 questions).
- L24-O2 (apply): predict the direction of heat, how each object's temperature changes, and when
  the net transfer stops, using particle collisions. Families F04, F05, F06 (6 questions).
- L24-O3 (relate): use a particle model to explain how gaining thermal energy melts, evaporates or
  sublimes a substance, and how losing it freezes, condenses or deposits one. Families F03, F07,
  F08, F09, F10 (10 questions).
- L24-O4 (apply): use melting and boiling points, particle attractions and heating or cooling
  curves to predict state changes (at a stated pressure) and to explain why the temperature holds
  steady while a substance melts, freezes, boils or condenses. Families F08, F10, F11, F12
  (8 questions).

## Review flags and resolutions
- L24-RF1 (pp. 308, 309, 312, 313, 318): OCR is empty or garbled on the image-heavy pages, and the
  scan cuts off the bottom lines of the spread. In the p. 313 cooling panel both thermometers read
  0 °C while the text calls each pot warmer than the ice. → All pages were rendered, and the spread
  and Key Science Concept content was read from the images. The truncated sentences are not relied
  on; their claim is cited from pp. 310–311. No game text gives the ice's temperature.
- L24-RF2 (brief check pp. 318–319, with pp. 312, 314, 316, 317): the Key Science Concept and the
  section 2 opener make it sound as if added energy always speeds particles up and raises the
  temperature during a change of state. → Game text says the temperature of a pure substance holds
  steady while it melts, freezes, boils or condenses at constant pressure, because the energy goes
  into freeing or binding particles; it does not claim this for evaporation below the boiling
  point. Tested by Q21, Q22 and Q23; stated in key idea 5 and Explorer O3/O4. The six-arrow model
  and its + Heat / − Heat labels are correct and kept (as a table). (OpenStax Chemistry 2e 10.3.)
- L24-RF3 (pp. 309, 310, 311, 319): the summary calls temperature a measure of the average *speed*
  of the particles (the p. 310 margin note says temperature is "related to" average speed), and
  p. 311 explains heat as fast particles of one object hitting slow ones of another. → Game uses average kinetic energy, and collisions between different objects are
  "more energetic" vs "less energetic". "Faster on average" is used only for one substance at two
  temperatures (Q02, Q07, Q08, Q10 choice f, Q24). Q09, Q12, key idea 3 and Explorer O2 were
  repaired in reviser round 1. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e 9.5.)
- L24-RF4 (pp. 314–317, 319): 0 °C, 100 °C, −269 °C and dry ice subliming are given without
  pressure conditions, and p. 317 says molten metal forms a solid as it cools to room temperature.
  → Every claim states normal (sea-level) or equal pressure; pressure effects on boiling are tested
  in L23 (Q26 rejected). Q29 and Explorer O4 teach that aluminum freezes at its melting point, about
  660 °C. The helium figures are verified. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e 10.3 and 10.4; LANL "Helium" and
  "Aluminum".)
- L24-RF5 (pp. 315–316): p. 315 says a particle can be sped "past the boiling point", Figure 2B's
  "typical particle" wording simplifies boiling, p. 316 says condensation happens at 100 °C, and
  the Figure 3A caption says the "fast-moving" particles stick together. → Game teaches evaporation
  at the surface at many temperatures and boiling with vapor bubbles throughout at the boiling point
  (Q24, Q25). Condensation is taught as vapor losing energy to something cooler and slowing down
  before it sticks, and no condensation temperature is tested (Q17). (USGS evaporation and
  condensation pages; OpenStax 10.3.)
- L24-RF6 (pp. 309–311): section 1 counts only motion energy as thermal energy and says only the
  bowl could thaw the peas, and the Figure 1 caption says even the cup has more thermal energy than
  the peas with no amounts given. → Questions compare thermal energy only when temperature is the
  same (Q01), the amount is the same (Q03), or the amounts differ greatly and are stated (Q02: 20×;
  Q06: an iceberg and a mug). Q13 asks how much energy each of three samples can give while cooling
  to 0 °C; its 0 °C tank has the most thermal energy but gives none. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e 5.1.)
- L24-RF7 (pp. 309, 314, 315, 317): mixed word forms (sublimate/sublime, deposit/deposition,
  condense/condensation). → Game uses the Key Science Concept's -ing names for changes and the
  vocabulary verbs in the glossary.
- L24-RF8 (p. 315): the dry-ice photo shows white fog beside a caption about solid turning to gas;
  the fog is liquid water droplets, and carbon dioxide gas is colorless. → No L24 text says the fog
  is carbon dioxide; Q16 describes only the shrinking solid. The fog is tested in L23-Q23. (ChemEd X
  dry-ice cloud; NLM MeSH "Carbon Dioxide".)
