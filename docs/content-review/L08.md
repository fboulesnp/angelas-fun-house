## Review flags and resolutions
- **L08-RF1** (p. 101; contradicts pp. 96–97 and p. 103)
  - Issue: the Key Science Concept callout says an eroded surface, a fault or an intrusion is *older* than any stratum it cuts. That reverses the law of cross-cutting relationships. The OCR layer misses this callout, so it was confirmed from a 250 dpi render.
  - Resolution: content teaches that the cutting feature is always younger, eroded surfaces included. Q12 repairs the reversed wording, and Q25 applies the rule to an eroded canyon; both carry external evidence. The O2 card warns that some printed diagrams flip the rule.
  - Sources: NPS, "Geologic Principles—Cross-cutting Relationships"; PRI Digital Atlas of Ancient Life, "Relative age dating"; textbook pp. 96, 97, 103.
- **L08-RF2** (pp. 98, 101, 103)
  - Issue: the textbook says strata with the same kinds of fossils are the same age, with no qualifier.
  - Resolution: content says *about* the same age, within the time the species lived, best narrowed by short-lived, widespread index fossils (Q17, Q18, Q19). The textbook's own p. 98 caption says "approximately the same age." The name "law of fossil succession" is kept (geologists more often say faunal succession).
  - Sources: Geosciences LibreTexts (UC Davis GEL 109), "Biostratigraphy – Biozones and Zone Fossils"; USGS, "Geologic Time: Relative Time Scale."
- **L08-RF3** (p. 103 vs p. 94)
  - Issue: the summary's "the layers on either side of the gap are the same age" is broader than Section 3, which is about *matching* layers.
  - Resolution: Q07 and Q08 talk about matching layers and add that the gap is younger.
  - Source: textbook p. 94.
- **L08-RF4** (pp. 91, 92, 102–103)
  - Issue: absolute dating is presented as igneous-only, while the vocabulary defines it for rocks, fossils and events. The lesson never explains how sedimentary layers get ages in years.
  - Resolution: content says radiometric dating works directly on igneous rock, and that sedimentary layers get age *ranges* by bracketing (Q24). Q23 explains why sand grains carry older clocks. Carbon-14 and other methods are outside this lesson and are not mentioned.
  - Sources: USGS, "Geologic Time: Radiometric Time Scale"; PRI, "Absolute age dating."
- **L08-RF5** (p. 93)
  - Issue: Grand Canyon numbers are rounded or garbled ("nearly 40 or more layers," "between 1.7 to 2 billion years"), and the record has gaps.
  - Resolution: not tested. The O1 card uses NPS figures (oldest rocks about 1.8 billion years, Kaibab rim rock about 270 million years) and mentions missing chapters.
  - Source: NPS, "Geology - Grand Canyon National Park."
- **L08-RF6** (pp. 98–99)
  - Issue: foraminifera are described in the past tense and as "animals," but they are living single-celled protists. Ammonites are called "shellfish" (they were extinct shelled cephalopods). Trilobite side facts were not checked further.
  - Resolution: no question tests group descriptions or trilobite dates. The O3 card says foraminifera still live today.
  - Sources: UCMP, "Introduction to the Foraminifera"; UCMP, "The Cephalopoda."
- **L08-RF7** (p. 97)
  - Issue: "the largest faults occur along transform boundaries" is questionable; subduction megathrusts are giant faults that produce the largest earthquakes.
  - Resolution: not used anywhere.
  - Source: USGS, "Introduction to Subduction Zones: Amazing Events in Subduction Zones."
- **L08-RF8** (pp. 91, 103)
  - Issue: the textbook gives a 4.6-billion-year Earth and says some igneous rocks are more than 4 billion years old. USGS gives 4.54 billion years; the oldest known rocks (about 4.03 billion years) are gneiss, and zircon crystals reach about 4.3 billion years.
  - Resolution: the O4 card says roughly 4.5 billion years and credits zircon crystals. Not tested.
  - Source: USGS, "Geologic Time: Age of the Earth."
- **L08-RF9** (pp. 90, 100–102)
  - Issue: OCR gaps (opener photo, KSC callouts, Figure 7 labels).
  - Resolution: all pages read from renders; no content relies on OCR alone.
- **L08-RF10** (p. 102)
  - Issue: the radiometric clock model leaves out the closed-system assumption.
  - Resolution: Q20 and Q21 state a closed-system condition, Q21's limitation names potassium-40's two decay products and argon loss, and the O4 card's `watchOut` names the assumption.
  - Sources: PRI, "Absolute age dating"; USGS, "Radiometric Time Scale."
