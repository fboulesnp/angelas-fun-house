## Objectives
- L14-O1 (observe): identify system and surroundings; use warming, cooling or light to read the
  direction of energy flow and classify the change. Families F01, F02, F03, F04 (8 questions).
- L14-O2 (relate): explain net transfer with bonds (breaking takes energy in, forming gives it out)
  and with energy being moved and stored, never created. Families F04, F05, F06, F07 (8 questions).

  data to judge a design and pick the next test. Families F10, F11, F12, F13 (8 questions).

## Review flags and resolutions
- L14-RF1 (p. 179, brief-flagged): the cold-pack equation is wrong.
  - Issue: the text gives NH₄Cl + H₂O + energy → NH₄OH + HCl. Figure 3 prints 2NH₄OH, which does
    not balance. Neither describes a cold pack. Dissolving ammonium chloride mainly separates it into
    NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ ions in water, an endothermic process. (Round 2 changed "only" to "mainly" here and in
    the O3 watchOut: a small fraction of NH₄⁺ reacts with water, which makes the solution weakly
    acidic.)
  - Decision: the page's placement rule (energy left = in, energy right = out) and the balanced
    iron equation are sound and kept.
  - Resolution: the NH₄OH + HCl equation is never shown. The game writes
    NH₄Cl + energy → NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻. Q18 asks the learner to choose it over the wrong-side, chlorine-gas
    and water-product versions. (Round 1 replaced the loose-atom version, which passed a simple atom
    count.) The O3 card's watchOut warns that some printed cold-pack equations are wrong.
  - Sources: OpenStax Chemistry 2e 11.1 (its cold-pack example is ammonium nitrate and its
    dissociation example is potassium dichromate; the supports texts now say so); PubChem ammonium
    chloride record, solubility section ("strongly endothermic").
- L14-RF2 (p. 177): the text says "ammonium and chlorine particles" and calls dissolving a
  reaction.
  - Resolution: the game says chloride ions and adds a glossary entry for ion. Q18 has a chlorine-gas
    distractor. Questions never ask whether dissolving is chemical or physical.
  - Sources: OpenStax 11.1 and 5.1.
- L14-RF3 (p. 174): the text says firework metals react with oxygen in the air. Fireworks carry
  their own oxidizer.
  - Resolution: fireworks appear only as an example of energy leaving as light, sound and heat. No
    question asks where the oxygen comes from.
  - Sources: Science in School, "The chemistry of fireworks", the supporting material for the
    Issue 21 article "Smoke is in the air: how fireworks affect air quality" (record title corrected
    in round 1); Texas A&M University at Galveston newsroom (2025).
- L14-RF4 (pp. 176, 178): p. 176 says any reaction needing continuous energy is endothermic. As an
  absolute rule this is unsafe, because exothermic reactions can need a starting input.
  - The p. 178 spread was checked and is consistent with the guardrail: bond breaking absorbs energy
    and bond forming releases it.
  - Resolution: the game classifies by net transfer only. Q12 repairs the misconception and Q13
    models the match. Q17 states the net direction in its prompt instead of relying on the rule.
  - Round 1: both reviewers found that the O1 Explorer card, its table and keyIdeas[2] still used
    "a heat source must keep feeding energy in" as a test. All three were rewritten: the card now
    says to compare energy in with energy out, and cooking is labeled as the lesson's model.
  - Sources: OpenStax 12.5 and 7.5.
- L14-RF5 (pp. 176, 178): the treatment of cooking as endothermic reactions is simplified.
  - Resolution: cooking is used only as the lesson models it. Q15 asks only about the energy taken
    in by the cooking reactions themselves, and no question quantifies the energy split.
  - Round 1: the O2 card no longer says cooked food "holds energy that raw food did not" (that
    invited the idea that cooking adds food Calories). It now says the absorbed energy is stored in
    the new substances only within the lesson's cooking model.
- L14-RF6 (pp. 180–181): the text gives 41 °C for the doubled-iron prototype on both pages, but the
  p. 180 photo shows 31 °C. Room temperature (about 25 °C) is inferred from "28 °C, three degrees

  - Resolution: use the written values. Q23 and Q24 say so, and no item refers to the photo.
- L14-RF7 (pp. 175, 179): the iron hand warmer and its equation 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ are a
  simplified model, since real packs also contain water and other helpers.
  - Resolution: the equation is used only as that model. Q14 keeps the point that the reaction, not

  - Source: OpenStax 5.2.
- L14-RF8 (p. 177): the text says the surrounding air's temperature drops. Strictly, the pack's own

  - Resolution: every page was rendered and all formulas were taken from the renders.
