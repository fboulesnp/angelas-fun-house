## Review flags and resolutions
- **L06-RF1** (pp. 65, 72; the brief's p. 72–73 check)
  - Issue: the Key Science Concept text says energy "cycles" through an ecosystem, and the Introduction says energy and matter are recycled.
  - Decision: energy flows one way and leaves as heat; only matter cycles. The spread's arrows themselves are correct (food to eater, and every organism to decomposers).
  - Resolution: Q11 sorts matter-only, energy-only and shared statements, and Q12 repairs the "energy cycles" wording.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 46.2 and 46.3; textbook p. 71.
- **L06-RF2** (pp. 70, 72–73)
  - Issue: the text says matter is "lost" moving up a food web.
  - Resolution: content says matter leaves the food chain as wastes, as respired carbon dioxide and water, or in remains that go to decomposers. Q10 and Q14–Q16 carry "destroyed" and "turned into energy" as distractors.
  - Sources: OpenStax 46.3; textbook p. 70.
- **L06-RF3** (p. 71)
  - Issue: the textbook presents 10 percent as exact.
  - Resolution: questions state "use the 10 percent model" as a condition. Q20 computes 12 percent from example data, and the O3 card calls 10 percent an average.
  - Sources: National Geographic Education "Energy Flow and the 10 Percent Rule"; OpenStax 46.2 (the Silver Springs example is about 14.5 percent).
- **L06-RF4** (p. 72)
  - Issue: the text says "populations get smaller at each level." Pyramids of numbers, and some aquatic biomass pyramids, can be inverted; energy pyramids (energy passed up per area per year) cannot.
  - Resolution: Q18 uses an oak tree that feeds thousands of caterpillars. Round 1: content now says the energy *passed up* to a level always shrinks (not the energy a level "holds"). Q16 now compares new biomass built over a year, with a limitation naming the inverted open-ocean plankton snapshot.
  - Source: OpenStax 46.2 (English Channel inverted biomass pyramid; upright energy pyramids in kcal/m²/yr).
- **L06-RF5** (p. 70)
  - Issue: the text calls zooplankton "single-celled consumers." Many are small animals, and the book's own art shows crustaceans.
  - Resolution: the glossary describes zooplankton as tiny drifting consumers, many of them small animals. No question tests whether they are single-celled.
  - Source: NOAA National Ocean Service, "What are plankton?"
- **L06-RF6** (pp. 66, 67, 69)
  - Issue: the text calls the red-tailed hawk a top predator that nothing eats, but Figures 1A and 2 draw a hawk → owl arrow. The hawk-disease example calls the rattlesnake increase "indirect", but Figure 1A shows hawks eating rattlesnakes, which would make it direct.
  - Resolution: neither claim is tested. Direct and indirect effects use original webs with every arrow stated, and the O1 card notes that "top predator" depends on the model.
  - Sources: internal textbook inconsistency, read at 300 dpi.
- **L06-RF7** (p. 74)
  - Issue: the text says the algae themselves consumed the oxygen. Algae do respire, especially at night, but in nutrient-polluted water the largest drain is usually decomposers breaking down dead algae. The sentence is incomplete rather than wrong.
  - Resolution: Q28 is keyed to decomposition; its explanation (round 1) adds that living algae also use oxygen at night, which is the part the textbook mentions.
  - Sources: NOAA National Ocean Service, "What is a dead zone?"; USGS Water Science School, "Water Q&A: What causes fish kills?"
- **L06-RF8** (pp. 74–75)
  - Issue: the textbook says the stocked predator was pike, says fish had become hard to find, and tells the fix as two separate steps. The scientists' review (full text read) reports: sewage diversion in the mid-1970s was the main driver of recovery; over a million juvenile pikeperch were stocked in 1984–1991, before and during the 1989–1993 mass fish removal; the removal cut planktivorous roach and smelt by about 90 percent (roach were abundant, open-water predators scarce); phosphorus left the lake in harvested fish; the phytoplankton change was not driven by extra zooplankton grazing, and the mechanisms remain unclear.
  - Resolution: questions say "predatory fish". Lake items present the cascade as the model's prediction or the textbook's account (Q24, Q25 and Q27 limitations or framing). The O4 card and key idea 8 were corrected in round 1.
  - Source: Salonen et al. 2020, *Hydrobiologia* 847: 4601–4619, open-access PDF at Natural Resources Institute Finland (Luke) Jukuri (the repository record page returned HTTP 502 to a reviewer, so the PDF URL is cited).
- **L06-RF9** (pp. 68–69)
  - Issue: the text calls earthworms and dung beetles decomposers (often called detritivores) and says the matter would be locked away "forever."
  - Resolution: questions use only bacteria and fungi as decomposer examples, and "forever" is not used.
  - Source: National Geographic Education, "Decomposers."
- Textbook figures (bison 500 kg and up to 8 kg of grass a day; Lahti dates) are treated as the textbook's examples, not as timeless facts. Q14 says "In the textbook's example."
