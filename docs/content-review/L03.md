## Review flags and resolutions
- **L03-RF1** (pp. 34, 38): the Key Science Concept says the 2.7 g/cm³ sample is "too dense to be made of silver". Silver is about 10.5 g/cm³, so the sample is far less dense than silver. The arithmetic and the aluminum conclusion are right.
  - Resolution: Q26 asks which results rule out silver; its misconception text and the O5 card's watch-out name the error. No game text repeats "too dense".
  - Sources: textbook pp. 34, 38; PubChem.
- **L03-RF2** (pp. 36, 38): metals "dissolving" in acids (silver in nitric acid, platinum in aqua regia, aluminum in three acids) are chemical reactions that make new substances, unlike sugar dissolving in water.
  - Resolution: solubility items use only sugar/flour and marker inks; no acid test appears in any question; the O4 card's watch-out explains the difference. I could not open a source on aluminum in nitric acid, so that detail is neither used nor corrected.
  - Sources: OpenStax 4.2 (metal + acid redox, Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂) and 1.3 (sugar dissolving is a physical change).
- **L03-RF3** (pp. 33, 35, 39): identifying properties are said to "never change". Boiling point depends on air pressure: water boils at about 90 °C in Leadville, Colorado. Odor is a weak identifier, and many substances have none.
  - Resolution: prompts say "pure" and "same conditions" or "ordinary air pressure"; Q18 repairs the idea; the glossary's boiling-point entry names sea-level pressure; no question uses odor as an identifier.
  - Source: OpenStax 10.3 (normal boiling point at 1 atm; Leadville example).
- **L03-RF4** (pp. 31, 33): steel beams, glass and "copper" pennies are presented as single substances. Steel is an alloy; glass has several ingredients; pennies are copper alloys or copper-plated zinc. The rendered p. 33 photos show green corrosion on both the statue and the coins.
  - Resolution: the O1 card's watch-out names these as mixtures; Q05 uses imagined pure-copper objects and says so in its `limitations`.
  - Sources: OpenStax 3.3 (steel is an alloy) and 1.2 (glass with added elements; pure-substance definition); U.S. Mint Coin Classroom (penny is copper over a zinc core); American Ceramic Society (the statue's green surface comes from copper oxidizing in air and sea spray).
- **L03-RF5** (p. 37): the text says flammable gases "will always ignite" and that a non-flammable gas may put a splint out. Ignition depends on the mix with air, and oxygen relights a glowing splint.
  - Resolution: Q22 describes hydrogen already mixed with air and asks only which property is tested; no gas procedure is given.
  - Sources: RSC "Combustion of hydrogen in air" (best pop at 20–40% hydrogen) and "The preparation and properties of oxygen".
- **L03-RF6** (p. 37): octane is called "the main component of gasoline"; gasoline is a blend.
  - Resolution: neither gasoline nor octane appears in any question or card.
  - Sources: EIA "Gasoline explained"; OpenStax 1.2 (gasoline is a homogeneous mixture).
- **L03-RF7** (pp. 34, 35, 38): number check.
  - Every textbook value matches PubChem within rounding: Ag 10.5 g/cm³ and about 960 °C; Pt 21.5; Pd 12.0 and about 1550 °C; Al 2.7 and 660 °C.
  - Magnesium (1.74 g/cm³, 650 °C) and gold (19.3 g/cm³, 1064 °C) are not in the textbook. They come from PubChem, whose melting points are given in kelvin and were converted, and they are cited wherever they are used (Q17, Q24, Q25, O5 card).
- **L03-RF8** (pp. 31, 37): physical versus chemical property is never defined. Only the p. 37 caption calls flammability a chemical property.
  - Resolution: Q23 carries the distinction with external support. The glossary defines only "chemical property", the term the book uses.
  - Sources: OpenStax 1.3; RSC magnesium page (2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO).
