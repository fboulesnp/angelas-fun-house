## Review flags and resolutions
- **L03-RF1** (pp. 34, 38): the Key Science Concept says the 2.7 g/cm³ sample is "too dense to be made of silver". Silver is about 10.5 g/cm³, so the sample is far less dense than silver. The arithmetic and the aluminum conclusion are right.
  - Resolution: Q26 asks which results rule out silver, and the O5 card's watch-out names the error. No game text repeats "too dense". (Round 1: Q26's misconception field no longer mentions the textbook slip, because it is a book error, not a learner belief. Round 2: O5 paragraph 2 rules out palladium instead of silver, so the card no longer gives away Q26's data.)
  - Sources: textbook pp. 34, 38; PubChem.
- **L03-RF2** (pp. 36, 38): metals "dissolving" in acids (silver in nitric acid, platinum in aqua regia, aluminum in three acids) are chemical reactions that make new substances, unlike sugar dissolving in water.
  - Resolution: solubility items use only sugar/flour and marker inks; no acid test appears in any question; the O4 card's watch-out explains the difference, and since round 1 its diagram says solubility keeps the substance the same only "for true dissolving (sugar in water)". I could not open a source on aluminum in nitric acid, so that detail is neither used nor corrected. The science reviewer noted that concentrated nitric acid passivates aluminum; the change was optional and no source was opened in round 1, so RF2 is unchanged.
  - Sources: OpenStax 4.2 (metal + acid redox, Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂) and 1.3 (sugar dissolving is a physical change).
- **L03-RF3** (pp. 33, 35, 39): identifying properties are said to "never change". Boiling point depends on air pressure: water boils at about 90 °C in Leadville, Colorado. Odor is a weak identifier, and many substances have none.
  - Resolution: prompts say "pure" and "same conditions" or "ordinary air pressure"; Q18 repairs the idea; the rewritten Q16 compares boiling points at the same pressure; the glossary's boiling-point entry names sea-level pressure; no question uses odor as an identifier.
  - Source: OpenStax 10.3 (normal boiling point at 1 atm; Leadville example).
- **L03-RF4** (pp. 31, 33): steel beams, glass and "copper" pennies are presented as single substances. Steel is an alloy; glass has several ingredients; pennies are copper alloys or copper-plated zinc. The rendered p. 33 photos show green corrosion on both the statue and the coins.

  - Round 2: the second science reviewer's web search (dated 2026-10-17) found consistent reports, for example CoinNews and NBC, of a ceremonial final strike at the Philadelphia Mint on 12 November 2025, with pennies remaining legal tender. The reviewer marked this informational, needing no change. RF4's issue text records it, and the date stays uncited because none of those outlets is on the guide's preferred list.
  - Resolution: the O1 card's watch-out names these as mixtures; Q05 uses imagined pure-copper objects and says so in its `limitations`.
  - Sources: OpenStax 3.3 (steel is an alloy) and 1.2 (glass with added elements; pure-substance definition); U.S. Mint Coin Classroom (penny is copper over a zinc core); American Ceramic Society (the statue's green surface comes from copper oxidizing in air and sea spray).
- **L03-RF5** (p. 37): the text says flammable gases "will always ignite" and that a non-flammable gas may put a splint out. Ignition depends on the mix with air, and oxygen relights a glowing splint.

- **L03-RF9** (p. 32, new in round 1): the book says a cup of water holds "billions of trillions of atoms". A 250 mL cup is about 13.9 mol, about 8.4 × 10²⁴ molecules or 2.5 × 10²⁵ atoms: trillions of trillions, roughly 25 to 25,000 times more, depending on how many billions are meant. "Billions of trillions" (10²¹ up to just under 10²⁴ atoms) fits anything from a small drop to about two teaspoons of water; OpenStax's 0.03 g drop holds about 10²¹ molecules.
  - Round 2: the science reviewer found that "about ten thousand times too few" is true only at the low end of the range. The RF9 issue text and this bullet now give the 25-to-25,000 range. No learner text is affected.
  - Resolution: key idea 1 and the O1 card already said "trillions upon trillions"; Q02's explanation and evidence now say "trillions of trillions of water molecules". No game text repeats the understated figure.
  - Sources: textbook p. 32; OpenStax 3.1 (Avogadro's number, about 18 g per mole of water, the 0.03 g drop example).
- **L03-RF10** (p. 37, new in round 1): the book's magnesium wire bursts into flame when heated to find its melting point, yet the game uses magnesium's melting point (O5 card, O5 table, Q25).
  - Resolution: Q25 says the strip is heated in argon gas so it cannot burn (prompt and `conditions`), and O5 paragraph 3 says the comparison is "measured without air, because hot magnesium catches fire". No question asks a learner to melt or burn magnesium.
