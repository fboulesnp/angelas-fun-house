## Review flags and resolutions
- **L05-RF1** (pp. 59, 60, 63): the OCR garbles every formula and arrow, and the p. 59 Figure 3B art prints a stray "+" before the arrow.
  - Resolution: the equation was taken from the renders and hand-checked, and the stray plus is never reproduced. Since round 1, Q09 traces hydrogen into water, Q10 uses the 6 O₂-per-sugar ratio, and Q11 checks conservation of mass.
  - Sources: textbook p. 60 (KSC equation); OpenStax Biology 2e 3.2 and 8.1.
- **L05-RF2** (p. 59): the text says producers don't need to take in oxygen because photosynthesis already supplies it. That is wrong for roots, seeds and every plant cell at night.
  - Resolution: Q20 repairs it with flooded-root evidence. Q17 shows respiration continuing in the dark, using a plant in sterile sand so that soil microbes add no carbon dioxide. The O3 card names the issue.
  - Round 2: Q17 now asks how much carbon dioxide photosynthesis alone removed in the light (150 + 60 = 210 ppm each hour), which works only if respiration keeps running in the light. Because the Ohio State page covers only night, an Encyclopedia of the Environment article (Dizengremel, Université de Lorraine) was added for respiration by day and night.
  - The Ohio State page is cited only for what it says: non-green parts respire, and green parts respire at night. The "day and night" wording in the content is general knowledge, not attributed to that page.
  - Sources: University of Missouri Extension, "Too much moisture can hinder root development"; Ohio State University PlantFacts, "Respiration".
- **L05-RF3** (p. 58, Figure 3A): the text says "every cell" has mitochondria, "most unicellular organisms" have them, and respiration takes place in them.
  - Corrections: mature red blood cells lack mitochondria; bacteria and archaea have none; glycolysis happens in the cytoplasm.
  - Resolution: content says "most of" respiration happens in mitochondria, which plant cells, animal cells and many single-celled organisms have. Q14 is keyed to "most of the work". Since round 1, Q23 says respiration happens "mostly in the mitochondria", and Q24 names cellular respiration without placing all of it there.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 7.2 and 4.2; OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology 2e 18.3.
- **L05-RF4** (pp. 57, 59, 61, 63): the text says energy is "stored in bonds" and released "as the molecule is broken down", and that respiration "generates" energy. It also says producers can't use light energy directly.
  - Resolution: no game text says that breaking bonds releases energy or that energy is created. Q12 repairs "creates energy"; Q13 teaches the net picture (forming the stronger CO₂ and H₂O bonds releases more than breaking the old ones costs). The O2 card's watchOut names the shortcut.
  - Round 1: the p. 57 idea now appears only in its defensible form, light energy stored in sugar so it can travel to cells that aren't capturing light. This covers Q07's explanation, the O1 card's third paragraph and its watchOut, and Q16's choice b rationale.
  - Sources: OpenStax Chemistry 2e 7.5; OpenStax Biology 2e 6.3.
- **L05-RF5** (pp. 55–56): herbivores, including the acorn-eating squirrel, are called predators.
  - Resolution: content uses "consumer" throughout, and no question asks about predators.
  - Source: OpenStax Biology 2e 45.6, which treats herbivory as separate from predation.
- **L05-RF6** (p. 57, Figure 2): the table lists soil as a source of plant matter.
  - Resolution (round 1): Q03 item s6 (producers get the carbon for their bodies from carbon dioxide) carries the point. The O1 card table says "plus small amounts of soil minerals", and its watchOut explains it. Q04, the original plant-mass item, was rejected as a duplicate of L04-Q16, because L04 teaches plant mass.
  - Source: OpenStax Biology 2e 31.1 (carbon is most of a plant's dry mass).
- **L05-RF7** (p. 57): the text says consumers don't use the water they take in to build cells.
  - Resolution: Q05 asks only about carbon atoms. Since round 1, its drinking-water distractor and its limitation both say that water also becomes part of cells.
  - Source: USGS Water Science School, "The Water in You" (up to 60% of an adult body is water).
- **L05-RF8** (p. 61): the text says food is digested into small molecules in the stomach.
  - Resolution: no question names an organ. Q23 (now a baked potato) orders chewing, then digestion "finishing" the starch, then transport in the blood. The O4 card gives the corrected sequence: mouth, stomach, then the small intestine, where most nutrients are absorbed.
  - Source: NIDDK, "Your Digestive System & How It Works".
- **L05-RF9** (p. 62): the text says no animal can make cellulose, but tunicates do.
  - Resolution: content says "your body can't make cellulose". Since round 1, Q21 matches cellulose to "forms the tough walls of plant cells", so it no longer depends on the claim.
  - Sources: the OIST seminar page, supplemented in round 1 by the OIST Institutional Repository record of Inoue, Nakashima and Satoh (2019), *Genes* 10: 294. Its abstract says tunicates are the only metazoans that can synthesize cellulose.
  - The PMC copy of that paper (PMC6523144) returned a reCAPTCHA page and MDPI returned HTTP 403, so the repository record was cited instead.
- **L05-RF10** (p. 62): a fat is defined as storing energy "in the form of oils".
  - Resolution: the glossary says oils are fats that are liquid at room temperature, and no question tests it.
  - Round 2 addition: cell membranes are built mainly from phospholipids, close relatives of fats made partly from fatty acids. "Fats help build membranes" (glossary, Q21 r2, Q24 i5, following p. 62) is kept as a grade-level simplification, and no question says membranes are made of fat.
  - Source: OpenStax Biology 2e 3.3 (re-opened in round 2: phospholipids are major membrane constituents and, like fats, are built from fatty acid chains on a glycerol backbone).
- **L05-RF11** (p. 63): the mirror-image word equations can suggest one reaction running backward.
  - Resolution: the O5 card's watchOut explains that the equations summarize different multistep pathways in different places, and that the oxygen comes from water. Q25 compares the gases and who carries out each process; Q26 compares energy and carries a limitation saying the equations are summaries.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 8.2 and 7.2.
- **L05-RF12** (pp. 56, 63; new in round 1): consumers are defined as organisms that can't make food by photosynthesis, and producers only by capturing sunlight. Chemosynthetic producers, such as deep-sea vent bacteria, make food without light and are not consumers.
  - Resolution: the glossary, key idea 1 and Q01 now define a consumer as an organism that can't make its own food. The glossary says most producers, not all, use sunlight. Q03's conditions limit it to typical producers and consumers.
  - Round 2: the science reviewer found that key idea 2 ("Producers build that sugar ... using light") and the photosynthesis glossary entry ("plants, algae and other producers") still said every producer uses light. Key idea 2 and the photosynthesis glossary entry were narrowed to photosynthetic producers in science review round 2: "Plants and other photosynthetic producers" and "plants, algae and some bacteria". Q02's read-aloud prompt no longer says producers make food "by photosynthesis".
  - Source: NOAA Ocean Exploration, "What is the difference between photosynthesis and chemosynthesis?"
- **L05-RF13** (p. 62; new in round 2): two oversimplifications. The page explains the name "simple carbohydrate" by the small number of atoms in a sugar; sugars are called simple because each is a single sugar unit, or two joined. It also defines a carbohydrate as an energy-storage molecule, though cellulose, named on the same page, is structural.
  - Resolution: the glossary defines a carbohydrate as storing energy or building structure, the O4 card calls complex carbohydrates long chains of sugars, and since round 2 the O4 card says "many" carbohydrates are energy molecules. No question uses either textbook wording, and Q21 matches cellulose to plant cell walls.
  - Source: OpenStax Biology 2e 3.2 (re-opened in round 2: monosaccharides are the simple sugars; polysaccharides are long chains of them; cellulose makes up most of a plant cell wall).
- Brief guardrail ("breathing is not identical to cellular respiration"): taught in the O3 card and tested in Q18 and Q19, with breathing distractors in Q13 and Q15. Q17's breathing distractor was removed when it was rewritten in round 2.
  - Round 2: breathing now has one definition everywhere: animals with lungs or gills move air or water over them so gases can be exchanged. This covers key idea 5, the O3 card's paragraph 3 and watchOut, Q18's key rationale and explanation, and Q19's i5 rationale.
  - Before, the lesson called breathing "gas exchange" while also saying a plant "never breathes", even though plant cells exchange gases.
