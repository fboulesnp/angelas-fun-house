## Review flags and resolutions
- L02-RF1: p. 22 says exactly 92 elements occur in nature, but the count depends on convention. → Content says "fewer than a hundred kinds of atoms", and no question tests the count (brief requirement); Q29 also says "fewer than a hundred". (Scientific American, Seaborg's 94)
- L02-RF2: OCR is empty or garbled on pp. 20, 24, 25, 27 and 28, including every subscript. → All pages rendered; every value used was read from the renders, and the vitamin C model was counted from the p. 20 render. (PubChem formulas)
- L02-RF3: the Figure 3A caption (p. 26) says quartz is 1 O : 1 Si, which contradicts the main text's correct 1 Si : 2 O. → Content uses 1:2 (SiO₂). Q18 carries the 1:1 version as unmatched distractor r6 and now explains the slip in its feedback; Q19 computes silicon from oxygen at 1:2. The O3 card names the caption error. (RSC, silicon; PubChem, silica O2Si)
- L02-RF4: p. 24 (and the Figure 2A photo) presents hydrogen peroxide on cuts as normal first aid, which is dated advice. → No first-aid or home-use content; hydrogen peroxide appears only as a different substance used as a disinfectant, never drunk. (Houston Methodist; MedlinePlus)
- L02-RF5: p. 25 calls isobutane "triangle-like" (text) and "pyramid-like" (figure label). → Content says "branched: one central carbon joined to the other three". (PubChem isobutane: propane with a methyl group at position 2)
- L02-RF6: p. 23's "line-angle formulas" description (every atom drawn with its symbol) actually describes structural formulas; skeletal drawings omit C and H. → The term is not used or tested. (OpenStax Organic Chemistry §1.12; OpenStax Chemistry 2e §2.4)
- L02-RF7: p. 23 says everyone uses the same colors for each element. This is a common convention (CPK), and carbon is often gray rather than black. → Colors are called a convention, diagrams label atoms by symbol, and Q05 tests that colors are labels. (Imperial College, Atom Colours)
- L02-RF8: pp. 27–29 simplify polymers. They call the subunit "always a molecule" and say polyester repeats "ester molecules", yet a polymer chain is usually counted as one macromolecule. Proteins and DNA mix different units in a set order, so they have no single ratio. → The lesson's model is kept. Only synthetic polymers illustrate repeating units. Round 1 removed "a protein" from the polymer glossary entry, because it contradicted this flag. Proteins and DNA appear only as natural-polymer examples (Q23, the O3 card, the Q24 hint). Q17's prompt now names its five structures, so proteins and DNA are outside it. The O3 card's watch-out names the issue. (LibreTexts polymer definition; OpenStax Organic Chemistry §31.4; OpenStax Biology 2e §3.4 and §3.5)
- L02-RF9: pp. 26 and 28 call salt's particles atoms, but they are ions. → "Atoms" is kept, since MS-PS1-1's assessment boundary excludes the ionic nature of subunits. The O3 card mentions ions. NaCl is consistently treated as a ratio, not a molecule (Q15, Q16, Q18). Q16 and Q18 speak of solid salt, because NaCl ion pairs exist in hot vapor. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e §2.6; NGSS MS-PS1-1)
- L02-RF10: p. 28 says cholesterol is arranged in "six-sided rings". Its steroid core has three six-membered rings and one five-membered ring. → Only the atom counts (C₂₇H₄₆O, 74 atoms) are used. (OpenStax Organic Chemistry §27.6; PubChem)
- L02-RF11: p. 29 makes absolute claims: snowflakes "always have six sides" and every salt grain is the same cube. → Content says "tend to", and objective O4 now says "six-fold symmetry" and "cube-like". Q27 and Q28 carry limitations, and the O4 card names the overstatement. (NOAA NESDIS; Libbrecht, Caltech SnowCrystals.com field guide; LibreTexts Mineralogy, halite)
- L02-RF12: p. 29 says diamond is "bonded in a cubic shape", and p. 26 calls its subunit a "cage". → Content says each carbon is locked to neighbors in every direction in a rigid 3D network. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e §10.5)
- L02-RF13: p. 22 lists hydrogen among the elements in the air we breathe; H₂ is only about 0.55 ppm of dry air. → Not used anywhere. (NASA Earth Fact Sheet)
- L02-RF14 (new, round 1): p. 29 says diamonds are good cutting tools for steel. Diamond is hard enough, but at cutting temperatures its carbon reacts with the iron (for example, turning to graphite with iron as a catalyst), so it wears quickly on steel; cubic boron nitride is widely used for machining and grinding ferrous metals. → Key idea 8, the O4 card and the O4 table no longer say diamond cuts steel. Q26 asks for a "very hard" edge, says diamond-tipped blades can cut very hard materials including steel, and has a limitation naming chemical wear and cubic boron nitride. (NIST: Lane, Dow and Scattergood, Wear 2013, abstract; Google Patents US 4,563,114; Fujisaki et al. 2009, Hokkaido University HUSCAP)
- L02-RF15 (new, round 1): p. 23 says stronger bonds are drawn with several sticks and weaker bonds with dotted sticks. Several sticks mark double or triple bonds, which are stronger only than a single bond between the same two atoms. Dotted lines usually mark much weaker attractions such as hydrogen bonds, often drawn between molecules. → Not used in content: every model uses single sticks only. (OpenStax Chemistry 2e §7.3, §7.5, §10.1)
- L02-RF16 (new, round 1): p. 21 calls a vitamin C molecule "millions of times smaller" than a printed period and "one of the smaller molecules". The comparison depends on whether width or volume is meant and on the print size, and a 20-atom molecule is small but not among the smallest (O₂ has 2 atoms, water 3). → Not used in content. No external source giving a period's width was found, so the flag states no figure of its own; its evidence is the textbook's own pages (21, 22, 28).
