## Review flags and resolutions
- L18-RF1 (pp. 232, 233, 234, 238, 240–241): OCR empty or garbled on the image-heavy pages, and the
  NGSS box on p. 233 is letter-spaced (p. 233 added to the flag's pages in round 2). → All pages
  rendered; spread content, arrows and Figure 3A forces taken from the images.
- L18-RF2 (pp. 235, 240): section 1 says liquid water leaves through the stomata and then
  evaporates; water actually evaporates inside the leaf and leaves as vapor (as the p. 240 caption
  says). → Game says vapor escapes through stomata; Q07 tests it. Since round 1, Q07 is the only
  item carrying the correction (Q04's stomata item was removed, because a reader of p. 235 could
  sort it as liquid). (OpenStax Biology 2e 30.5.)
- L18-RF3 (pp. 237, 241, 243): "crystallization" covers both gas → solid and liquid → solid (gas →
  solid is usually called deposition); "crystals ... made of atoms" (ice is made of water
  molecules); the summary's "crystallized water vapor that has condensed" muddles the changes. →
  Keep the textbook term and add the scientists' terms (glossary, Explorer O1). Q05's conditions
  name both freezing (liquid → solid) and deposition (gas → solid); Q01's frost item is the
  gas → solid case. Clouds are droplets, ice crystals or both. (NOAA "The water cycle"; NWS "Cloud
  Development".)
- L18-RF4 (p. 240): the spread says every process is powered by the sun or gravity; condensation
  and crystallization happen when vapor gives off energy as it cools. → Q11 leaves them out of the
  sun/gravity sort and says why; Q09 sorts by energy gained or given off; Explorer O2 names the
  limit. (NWS "Cloud Development"; USGS "Condensation and the Water Cycle".)
- L18-RF5 (p. 235): "about 90 percent" from water bodies and "10 percent" from transpiration are
  rough estimates; a small share also comes from sublimation. → Not tested; called "about" figures.
  (USGS "Evaporation and the Water Cycle".)
- L18-RF6 (p. 238): Figure 3A's size labels (< 2 mm, > 2 mm, > 5 mm) don't say whether they are
  diameters or radii, and the sources' breakup sizes differ. → Q14 tests only the agreed shape
  pattern (round → flattened underneath → breaks apart; not a teardrop), not size cutoffs. Since
  round 1, Explorer O2 and key idea 5 teach air resistance and drop flattening. (USGS "Are Raindrops
  Shaped Like Teardrops?"; NASA GPM "The Anatomy of a Raindrop".)
- L18-RF7 (pp. 234, 239, 240–241): the spread draws evaporation only from the ocean and one set of
  arrows; risk of reading it as a compulsory loop. → Q19 repairs "one fixed loop", Q22 asks how to
  read the missing lake evaporation, Explorer O3 says water need not visit every place. (Textbook
  pp. 234, 239; NOAA "The water cycle".)
- L18-RF8 (p. 243): "smaller holes and finer materials collect the most water" is too absolute;
  research found a best balance of fine strands and open spacing, and droplets flow around solid
  surfaces. MIT also says typical nets' strands and holes are much too large, so the textbook's
  direction holds for ordinary nets. → Q28 uses invented data showing an optimum; Explorer O4 says
  smaller holes help only up to a point and dates the 2 % / 10 % figures to the 2013 MIT study of
  mild fog; Q27 uses them as round values. (MIT News, 2013.)
- L18-RF9 (p. 242): dated or simplified figures and history ("over one million people", < 5 mm of
  rain per year, a "physics student" in the 1950s, running water "for the first time", nets used
  "all around the world"). → Not tested; numbers treated as of the time the textbook was written;
  fog nets framed as an extra water source that depends on fog and wind (Q25). (FAO "Capturing fog
  to make water".)
- L18-RF10 (p. 237, added in round 2): crystallization "occurs when the air temperature falls below
  freezing". Below 0 °C is needed but not enough: cloud droplets often stay supercooled liquid well
  below 0 °C (to about −40 °C in experiments) until a particle or ice crystal starts freezing. → Not
  tested; no game text says water must freeze below 0 °C. (Hong Kong Observatory "Supercooling and
  Superheating".)
