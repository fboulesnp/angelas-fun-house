## Review flags and resolutions
- **L11-RF1** (p. 147)
  - Issue: stromatolites are described as the remains of "algae colonies". They are built by cyanobacterial mats (once called blue-green algae).
  - Resolution: no question tests what built them. In round 1 they were removed from the O2 processes table, because a fossil's age is not how long a process takes. In the second review cycle the remaining sentence left O2 paragraph 3 (which defines time scale as how long a process takes) and became a chart-reading caution in the O2 watch-out: the chart's billions-of-years placement is how old the oldest fossils are, not how long one took to form. Content never calls them algae.
  - Sources: NPS, The Stromatolites of Glacier National Park.
- **L11-RF2** (pp. 141, 149)
  - Issue: uniformitarianism is worded as processes acting "the same way". Read literally, this implies constant rates.
  - Resolution: content says the same kinds of processes act through time, but rates can vary and sudden events are part of the record. Q24 keys exactly this distinction, and Q23 repairs the "uniform = unchanging" reading.
  - Sources: NPS, Geologic Principles—Uniformitarianism; Encyclopedia.com (Gale).
- **L11-RF3** (p. 144)
  - Issue: the textbook gives continental motion as "1–3 cm per year". Plates carrying continents move at different speeds; Australia, for example, moves about 7 cm per year. The USGS spreading rates (under 2.5 to over 15 cm/yr) measure how fast two plates separate, not how fast one continent moves. The second review cycle rewrote the issue this way, because the earlier text argued from spreading rates, which mixes two measures.
  - Resolution: content says "a few centimeters a year". Q16 uses a stated, steady 2.5 cm/yr, and its conditions describe the USGS range as seafloor-spreading rates; it needs no change.
  - Sources: USGS, Understanding plate motions; Geoscience Australia, GDA2020 (added in the second review cycle).
- **Textbook evidence for RF3–RF8:** since round 1, the `supports` text of each flag states the specific textbook claim being flagged, not a generic section summary.
- **L11-RF4** (p. 144)
  - Issue: the text says weight "cements" sand, which blends compaction and cementation.
  - Resolution: the glossary, the O1 card and its watch-out, and Q06 all separate the two steps: weight packs the grains, and groundwater minerals glue them.
  - Sources: NPS Zion, Sand Dunes to Sandstone.
- **L11-RF5** (p. 144)
  - Issue: the text says cave water reacts with limestone, but it doesn't say the water is weakly acidic.
  - Resolution: content and Q05 key "weakly acidic water dissolves the limestone". Q05's explanation uses the NPS maximum widening rate of about 1 mm per year.
  - Sources: NPS, Solution Caves.
- **L11-RF6** (p. 145)
  - Issue: eruptions are said to last "several weeks". Real eruptions range from under a day to thousands of years, with a median of about 7 weeks.
  - Resolution: content says "often weeks, some last years or far longer" (O3 table and key idea 6, widened in the second review cycle). No question tests eruption length. Q18's eruption item says "within weeks", and Q21's limitations state the range.
  - Sources: Oregon State University, Volcano World.
- **L11-RF7** (p. 145)
  - Issue: an asteroid is defined as "mountain-sized". Asteroids range from under 10 m to about 530 km across.
  - Resolution: content refers only to "a large asteroid impact". Asteroid size is not tested.
  - Sources: NASA, Asteroids: Facts.
- **L11-RF8** (p. 145)
  - Issue: flash floods are attributed only to heavy rain. A shared sentence also ties earthquakes to flash floods.
  - Resolution: content adds dam, levee and ice-jam releases, and ties earthquakes only to landslides. Q20 keys both heavy rain and the sudden release of held-back water.
  - Sources: NOAA National Weather Service (Central Illinois).
- **L11-RF9** (pp. 142–143)
  - Issue: the textbook's Namib figures don't match NASA's.
    - It places the desert in "western Africa" (p. 142). The desert lies on the southwest coast of Africa, mostly in Namibia.
    - It gives the largest dunes as 240 m. NASA puts the tallest at about 325 m.
    - It gives the desert as over 80,000 km² of "wind-blown sand". That figure appears to describe the whole Namib Desert, which also includes rocky plains; NASA gives about 34,000 km² for the Namib Sand Sea. So the real error is calling the whole area sand, not comparing two measures of one thing (reworded in the second review cycle).
    - Its 55 million years is an estimate of the age of the desert itself (NASA, citing the United Nations Environment Programme), not a dated age of the sand. (Verifier, 2026-10-14 record: the earlier gloss "meaning how long the region has been dry" was removed, because the NASA page does not say it.)
  - Resolution:
    - The textbook's figures are treated as rough examples. No question tests the area, the age or the exact dune height.
    - Q09 uses round, stated values: a 300 m dune "close to the height of the tallest Namib dunes". Its explanation gives both 240 m and 325 m.
    - The dunes are far younger than their grains. Dated Namib dunes are thousands of years old (oldest sand about 5,700 years in a large linear dune), while NASA gives Dune 45's sands as about 5 million years old.
    - Q17 now keys accumulation "over thousands of years or more", not millions.
  - Sources: NASA Earth Observatory, Namib Sand Sea; Bristow, Duller & Lancaster (2007), via the Aberystwyth research portal; NASA Earth Observatory, Namibia's Protected Coast (added in the second review cycle). Round 1 added p. 142 / PDF 178 to the textbook evidence.
- **L11-RF10** (pp. 144, 147)
  - Issue: the lesson gives three different uplift durations: 100 million years, "hundreds of millions" and about 30 million years for the Rockies.
  - Resolution: content says "millions to tens of millions of years". Q10 depends only on relative order, and the neighboring items are far apart in time.
  - Sources: textbook (inconsistency); NPS, Bighorn Canyon Mountain Building, for context.

  - Issue: the vocabulary box and Section 2 present uplift only as slow. Mountain ranges do rise slowly overall, but large earthquakes can raise land suddenly: in the 1964 Great Alaska Earthquake the fault ruptured in about 4 minutes and land rose by up to about 11 m on Montague Island.
  - Resolution: the uplift glossary entry, key idea 1 and the O1 card now say uplift is usually very slow. Q07, Q10 and Q18 test mountain-range uplift over millions of years, which stays slow, so they are unaffected.
