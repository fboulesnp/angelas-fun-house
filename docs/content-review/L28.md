## Review flags and resolutions
- **L28-RF1** (p. 377)
  - Issue: the text says that without pollinators, farmers could not produce the fruits, seeds, grains and nuts they grow. Staple grains (wheat, rice, corn) are wind- or self-pollinated. The same page lists oranges and green beans among bee-visited crops and says favorite foods rely on pollinators; green beans are self-pollinating, and Navel and Valencia oranges set fruit without pollination.
  - Resolution: content says "many" crops. Q08 rejects "every crop". Q09 sorts apples, squash, almonds and blueberries apart from wheat, rice and corn. No content or question uses oranges or green beans as pollinator-dependent.
  - Sources: National Research Council (2007), *Status of Pollinators in North America*, ch. 4 (re-opened; apples need cross-pollination between cultivars); Montana State University Extension, "Which Garden Vegetables and Fruits Need Insect Pollination"; University of Arizona Cooperative Extension, "Oranges for Southern Arizona"; textbook p. 377.
- **L28-RF2** (p. 379)
  - Issue: the text says Cavendish bananas are safe from Panama disease, and that yellow sigatoka leaf spot makes Cavendish plants sicken and die. Cavendish resisted only Race 1 of Panama disease and is susceptible to Tropical Race 4, the wilt now destroying plantations. Yellow Sigatoka is a leaf-spot disease that reduces photosynthesis and bunch size. The text also says Panama disease drove other varieties to extinction; Gros Michel was dropped by the export trade but is still grown by smallholders.
  - Resolution: content says "newer fungal diseases" and never names yellow sigatoka as the killer or claims an extinction. Q13–Q15 test only the uniformity-versus-variety principle; Q13 now asks about "the supply of these store bananas", not the world's, and Q14's key says wild traits help "develop new bananas".
  - Sources: Zheng et al. 2018, *Frontiers in Plant Science*; ProMusa, "Tropical race 4 - TR4", "Sigatoka leaf spot" and "Gros Michel"; textbook p. 379.
- **L28-RF3** (p. 381)
  - Issue: Figure 3's title asserts cause from two time series, and its caption claims a rabies rise that the graph does not show. Its numbers are estimates from the time of writing. The rabies-only story also simplifies later findings: comparing districts that had been vulture habitat with districts where vultures were rare, Frank and Sudarshan found human mortality rose by more than 4 percent where vultures vanished, through a broad sanitation shock. More dogs and rabies are one likely pathway. That study measured human deaths, not dogs.
  - Resolution:
    - No question reuses the graph numbers. Q19 reads a pattern from model data.
    - Q20 asks what evidence would support cause: a comparison of places with and without vulture loss, the kind of comparison Frank and Sudarshan made for human death rates. Its explanation no longer implies that the dog rise was shown this way.
    - The O3 watchOut separates correlation from cause and names the later findings and their several pathways.
  - Sources: Frank and Sudarshan 2024, *American Economic Review* 114(10) (AEA abstract page); Anthropocene Magazine on the habitat-versus-non-habitat comparison and rabies-vaccine sales; Markandya et al. 2008, *Ecological Economics* (University of Bath portal); University of Warwick Economics news, re-read; its "supports" text now describes only the before/after comparison in former vulture areas; textbook p. 381.
- **L28-RF4** (p. 381)
  - Issue: rabies is called "a fatal disease for humans" without qualification. It is virtually always fatal once symptoms appear but preventable with prompt care after exposure. Dogs cause about 99% of human cases.
  - Resolution: content calls rabies a dangerous disease spread mostly by dog bites and gives no medical advice. No question tests fatality.
  - Sources: WHO rabies fact sheet; textbook p. 381.
- **L28-RF5** (p. 383)
  - Issue: the text says flooding is now the leading cause of U.S. natural-disaster property loss and fatalities. FEMA supports "most common and most expensive". The NWS ranks heat as the top weather killer (2004–2013 averages: about 123 heat deaths versus 75 flood deaths a year).
  - Resolution: content says "among the most common and costly". No question tests the statistic.
  - Sources: FEMA NFIP FloodSmart, "Cost of Flooding"; NWS Omaha, "Heat Safety"; textbook p. 383.
- **L28-RF6** (pp. 384–385)
  - Issue: the New York City story is simplified.
    - The Catskill/Delaware supply has run under EPA filtration-avoidance determinations since 1993, kept through watershed protection. The water is still chlorinated.
    - The text frames the crisis as overloaded treatment plants. In fact the Catskill/Delaware supply was unfiltered, and the real choice was a new filtration plant (required under the Surface Water Treatment Rule) or watershed protection to qualify for filtration avoidance.
    - The p. 384 caption says "wetlands upstream" were protected. The watershed is mostly forest (about 70–90 percent), along with farms, streams, wetlands and reservoirs.
    - The population and volume figures date from the time of writing.
  - Resolution: content says most of the supply skips filtration but is still disinfected and monitored. Q12 and Q26 reject "no treatment needed". The O4 card, its table, key idea 6 and Q26 name "a huge new filtration plant" as the alternative. The O4 card and Q26 say forests, soils, streams and wetlands clean the water. The table's growth row is labelled as the lesson's argument about treatment. No question tests the numbers.
  - Sources: U.S. EPA Region 2 (archive), "History of the New York City Watershed Filtration Avoidance Determination"; National Academies (2020), *Review of the New York City Watershed Protection Program*, ch. 10; NYC DEP, "Filtration Avoidance Determination", "History of New York City's Drinking Water" and "Drinking Water"; textbook pp. 384–385.
  - Not used: the NYC Independent Budget Office report the science reviewer suggested (`ibo.nyc.ny.us/iboreports/waterreport.pdf`). The link and its press release now redirect to the IBO home page, so the report could not be opened. The EPA history document supports the same points.
- **L28-RF7** (p. 377)
  - Issue: decomposers are said to release "energy and matter" into the soil. This could suggest energy is recycled, and carbon largely returns to the air as CO₂.
  - Resolution: Q07 (rationales now say energy is passed along and changed, ending as heat, not "used up"), the O2 cycle diagram (decomposers and plants both release CO₂ by respiration) and the O2 watchOut all say matter cycles while energy leaves as heat.
  - Sources: OpenStax *Biology 2e* 46.3; textbook p. 377.
- **L28-RF8** (p. 382)
  - Issue: coastal marshes, swamps and dunes are said to "absorb water". Their main role is to act as barriers that take up wave energy and slow water.
  - Resolution: content uses the barrier wording. Q23 rejects "soak up all the floodwater", and its explanation adds that beach-grass leaves trap wind-blown sand (University of New Hampshire record).
  - Sources: NOAA National Ocean Service, "What is a living shoreline?"; textbook pp. 382–383.
- **L28-RF9** (pp. 374, 375, 378, 381, 383)
  - Issue: OCR gaps and garbling.
  - Resolution: every page was read from its render, and pp. 378 and 381 again at 200–220 dpi.
- **L28-RF10** (p. 385)
  - Issue: the text says the watershed plan saves money but gives no figures, and the brief requires fictional economic values.
  - Resolution: Q27 uses invented money for a fictional town. Q28 weighs values that a price leaves out. Q30 uses invented ratings and money for the made-up town of Gullport.
  - Sources: textbook pp. 378 and 385.
- **L28-RF11** (p. 377), new in reviser round 1
  - Issue: the text says building materials such as wood, stone, minerals and metals "come from Earth's ecosystems". Stone, metals and most minerals form through geological processes, not living things; they are natural resources but not ecosystem products. The p. 377 caption also implies the meadow's soil reaches the farm, although a farm's fertility comes from decomposers in its own soil.
  - Resolution:
    - Content separates resources grown by living ecosystems (wood, food, medicine chemicals) from stone and metals dug from rocks formed by geology. This covers key idea 1, the O1 card's third paragraph and the O1 table.
    - The O1 table's first row now lists only wheel labels plus wood and fresh water, marked as named in Section 1.
    - Q04's rationale and explanation are fixed. Q03 asks about services from healthy ecosystems near farms.
    - The O2 card says the same recycling keeps a farm's own soil fertile.
  - Sources: USGS Mineral Resources Program, Fact Sheet 2016–3034; textbook p. 377.
