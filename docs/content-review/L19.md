## Review flags and resolutions
- **L19-RF1** (pp. 245, 246, 253)
  - Issue: renewable is defined partly as a supply that "cannot be depleted", and geothermal heat is called "inexhaustible". The other clause ("replenished faster than it is used up") ties the label to the rate of use. The draft content mixed that rate definition with "renewable resources can be overused" (science review, round 1).
  - Resolution (updated in round 1):
    - Content defines renewable by replacement within about a human lifetime, which the p. 245 NGSS box supports.
    - Sustainable use is treated as a separate race between the rate of use and the rate of replacement.
    - Q02 targets "refills instantly". Q13 c and Q21 b target "never runs out".
    - Q13 uses The Geysers' 1980s steam decline.
  - Sources: EIA, Renewable energy explained; DOE Geothermal FAQs; Calpine, Recharging The Geysers.
- **L19-RF2** (pp. 247, 257, 259)
  - Issue: "most groundwater is nonrenewable" can be read as the forbidden absolute.
  - Resolution: groundwater is excluded from the sorting item (Q01 limitations). Q06 tests what controls refilling, and Q23 asks how much the farms may pump within recharge. The O1 table now says "depends on the aquifer", because rain, rock and depth set how fast an aquifer refills.
  - Sources: USGS, Groundwater Decline and Depletion; USGS, High Plains water-level changes.
- **L19-RF3** (p. 247)
  - Issue: "at least 2 million years" to refill the Great Artesian Basin is overstated.
  - Resolution: content says water in parts of the basin has been underground for more than a million years and that refilling is extremely slow. The figure is not tested.
  - Sources: Torgersen et al. (chlorine-36 dating) abstract.
- **L19-RF4** (p. 246)
  - Issue: fresh water is given as 3%; USGS says about 2.5%. The textbook is rounding rather than wrong.
  - Resolution: Q05 states that it uses the textbook's rounded values and gives the USGS figure in its conditions.
  - Sources: USGS, Where is Earth's Water?
- **L19-RF5** (p. 249)
  - Issue: the text says the arch "reduces the pressure on the dam", and that 21,000 workers "and their families" moved to the area.
  - Resolution: content says the curve redirects part of the water's push into the canyon walls, and the heavy base works by gravity. Worker numbers are not tested.
  - Sources: USBR, Hoover Dam FAQs.
- **L19-RF6** (p. 249)
  - Issue: the text says warmer water is released below the dam. Big Colorado River dams release cold water from deep in the reservoir.
  - Resolution: content says only that dams change downstream temperature. The O4 watch-out names Glen Canyon Dam's cold deep releases and, since round 1, adds that warmer surface water passes through when the reservoir is low. No question asks which way the temperature changes.
  - Sources: NPS, Grand Canyon's Native Fish (re-opened 2026-10-17).
  - The science reviewer's proposed wording "much colder in summer than it was before the dams" was not used. The NPS page does not compare temperatures with the time before the dams.
- **L19-RF7** (pp. 250–251)
  - Issue: the text says most electricity and transportation energy comes from oil. Oil supplies only about 0.7% of U.S. utility-scale electricity.
  - Resolution: content ties petroleum mainly to transportation, and nothing tests the electricity claim.
  - Sources: EIA, Electricity in the United States; EIA, Use of oil.
- **L19-RF8** (pp. 245, 251, 252, 254, 258, 259)
  - Issue: several numbers are dated, unverified or inconsistent: 910 L of water per phone, oil's share of world energy in 2017, Ghawar's output, The Geysers' share of electricity (60% in the text versus "more than half of the northern part of the state" in the caption), Ethiopia's growth rate, the "90% of U.S. forests" figure, and Canada's planting totals.
  - Resolution: none is tested. They are treated as dated examples (O3 watch-out).
  - Sources: Government of British Columbia, Reforestation.
- **L19-RF9** (p. 256)
  - Issue: the text says Chuquicamata's copper "formed more than 300 million years ago" and calls it "the largest" mine, though the caption says "one of the largest".
  - Resolution (updated in round 1): the age correction (about 31–35 million years ago) now lives only in this flag. It was removed from the O2 watch-out as untested trivia. Content never ranks the mine. Neither the age nor the ranking is tested.
  - Sources: Ballard et al. 2001; USGS, Porphyry Copper Deposit Model.
- **L19-RF10** (p. 259)
  - Issue: 12,000–15,000 km² of forest per year does not match "48 football fields per minute". FAO's figure of about 13 million ha (130,000 km²) per year does match it, so the textbook's area appears to be missing a zero.
  - Resolution: the rate is not tested or quoted in content.
  - Sources: FAO Newsroom, 25 March 2010.
- **L19-RF11** (p. 259)
  - Issue: renewability is assigned by species: redwood and cedar are called nonrenewable, and redwoods are said to live "up to 1,000 years". Coast redwoods resprout and can live about 2,000 years, and only about 4% of old growth remains.
  - Resolution: content and Q03 frame renewability as regrowth time versus harvest rate (old growth versus a replanted plantation), not as a property of a species.
  - Sources: NPS, Coast Redwood.
- **L19-RF12** (pp. 244–245, 247–248, 250–251, 253–258)
  - Issue: OCR is empty or garbled on these pages.
  - Resolution: every page was rendered, and the facts from figures, maps and the spread were taken from the renders.
- **L19-RF13** (pp. 247, 259; added in round 1)
  - Issue: the Figure 1B caption and the Lesson Summary tie groundwater locations mainly to rain and snow. The textbook's own map marks major aquifers under the Sahara (North West Sahara and Nubian Sandstone systems) and dry central Australia (Great Artesian Basin). Those aquifers hold water from wetter past climates, or water that enters far away at the basin edges.
  - Resolution: the O2 card (paragraph 3, table and alt text), key ideas and the Q07 explanation say groundwater collects wherever rain and snow soaked in, today or during wetter times long ago.
  - Sources: UN News (2013), Nubian Sandstone Aquifer System; Torgersen et al. abstract.
- **L19-RF14** (p. 248; added in round 1)
  - Issue: "earth and rockfill dams are not as strong, so they cannot store as much water" is stated as a general rule. The earth-fill Garrison Dam's Lake Sakakawea holds up to 24.2 million acre-ft, compared with Lake Mead's 28.9 million.
  - Resolution: the O4 card (paragraph 2, table and alt text) and Q25 present it as the team's judgment for this canyon site. Q26's key does not depend on the general claim.
  - Sources: USGS station 06338000 record; Reclamation Lake Mead FAQ.
- **L19-RF15** (pp. 250, 257; added in round 1)
  - Issue: "as the pressure increases, the temperature also increases" implies that pressure causes the heating. The spread also says petroleum deposits occur "wherever" marine remains collected.
  - Resolution: content says deeper burial raises both heat and pressure (Q09), and it uses "formed where", never "wherever". Neither wording is tested.
  - Sources: EIA, Geothermal explained.
