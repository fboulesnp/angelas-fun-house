## Objectives

  how ecological succession rebuilds a community step by step. Families F01, F02, F03, F04
  (9 questions).
- L22-O2 (relate): explain how a change to a living part of an ecosystem (prairie dogs) or a
  nonliving part (water, light, sediment) changes resources, raising some populations and lowering
  others. Families F05, F06, F07, F08, F13 (10 questions).

  predict connected consequences and judge claims about change and recovery. Families F04, F06,

## Review flags and resolutions
- **L22-RF1** (pp. 284, 285, 286, 291, 292)
  - Issue: OCR is empty or garbled on the image-heavy pages, including the p. 291 graph's bars.
  - Resolution: all pages were rendered. The graph was re-rendered at 250 dpi, and its bars were
    read as relative values with blue = before and gold = after, matching the panel frames. Q17 and
    Q18 say the values are estimates from an illustration.
- **L22-RF2** (p. 287)
  - Issue: the textbook says an eruption can kill all living things in the area. That is too
    absolute.
  - Resolution: the game says an eruption can kill most living things nearby, and that survivors
    and new arrivals both drive recovery. Q08 tests the role of survivors. Q07's stem no longer
    says the forest was "wiped out".
  - Source: USGS Mount St. Helens ecology page.
- **L22-RF3** (pp. 285–287)
  - Issue: succession is called "predictable", and Figure 1 has fixed year labels.
  - Resolution: the game calls succession a common pattern, not a timetable, with an end state that
    can differ. See the Q03 conditions and limitations, Q07 (its new choice f is a fixed 100-year
    timetable), and the Explorer O1 watch-out.
  - Source: OpenStax Biology 2e 45.6.
- **L22-RF4** (p. 289)
  - Issue: the textbook says prairie dogs make pronghorn decline. The NPS says pronghorn are drawn to
    prairie dog towns and benefit from them.
  - Resolution: pronghorn appear in no question. Q14 uses an unnamed shrub eater under stated
    conditions, and its learner-facing limitations no longer discuss pronghorn. The Explorer O2
    watch-out now names bison and pronghorn as grazers drawn to prairie dog towns and no longer
    talks about "the game". See also RF10.
  - Source: NPS Badlands prairie dog article.
- **L22-RF5** (p. 288)
  - Issue: the "2 percent of 100 years ago" figure is a dated, uncertain estimate (the NPS gives about
    5 percent of the original range). The burrow art labels a "jackrabbit", but the text says
    rabbits.
  - Resolution: neither is tested. The decline is described only as large.
  - Source: NPS.
- **L22-RF6** (p. 291)
  - Issue: the spread says cattle egrets "were introduced". In fact they crossed from Africa on their
    own in the 1870s–1880s and reached North America by about 1952, helped by farmland. The graph is
    stylized and has no units.
  - Resolution: questions describe egrets only as absent before and common after. The Explorer O3
    watch-out explains their arrival. Round 1: the glossary now defines an introduced species as one
    moved by people, on purpose or by accident, citing p. 287 instead of p. 291. By the game's own
    definition, then, the egrets are not introduced.
  - Sources: Audubon field guide and Audubon magazine; USDA NAL Thesaurus (added in round 1).
- **L22-RF7** (p. 292)
  - Issue: the Figure 4 label says the channel joins the Mississippi River, but the Wax Lake Outlet
    carries Atchafalaya River water (opened 1941). The 2.8 km²/yr rate is a combined figure for the
    Atchafalaya and Wax Lake deltas, from one estimate by Louisiana State University scientists that
    NASA reports. Wax Lake alone grows about 1.2 km²/yr, and growth varies with floods and hurricanes.
  - Resolution: Q25 names the Atchafalaya. Q26 now presents 2.8 km²/yr as "one scientific estimate"
    of the two deltas' combined rate (no longer "satellite studies") and states that real growth
    varies.
  - Sources: NASA Earth Observatory (two pages; "New Land" re-opened in round 1).
- **L22-RF8** (pp. 292–293)
  - Issue: the textbook frames Caernarvon as a wetland-rebuilding flood-protection project. CPRA
    states its goal as reducing saltwater intrusion in Breton Sound. Its objectives are marsh growth,
    less marsh loss and fish and wildlife productivity. It was built 1988–1991, and how much land it
    builds is debated.
  - Resolution: Q24 tests only the gate logic (open when safe, closed when floods threaten). CPRA's
    closures for flood advisories support that logic. No question gives a Caernarvon land figure.
  - Source: CPRA 2024 operations summary.
- **L22-RF9** (pp. 285, 287, 289)
  - Issue: "pioneer species" and "keystone species" describe ideas the lesson teaches, but the
    textbook doesn't use either term.
  - Resolution: both are in the glossary with original definitions. Round 1 removed the textbook
    notes from the definitions, and the keystone definition no longer shares a run of p. 289 wording.
    This flag now carries the note that the textbook doesn't use the names. No question requires
    either term.
  - Sources: OpenStax 45.6 and NPS.
- **L22-RF10** (pp. 288–289), new in round 1
  - Issue: p. 288 says burrows make grass more abundant for bison, and p. 289 says prairie dogs
    reduce flowering plants and shrubs. The NPS pages say something different. Clipping produces
    more nutritious regrowth, which draws bison and pronghorn, and clearing grass makes new space for
    forbs. Neither page says colonies hold more grass. Grass is itself a flowering plant. The shrub
    reduction is supported: in the five-year PLOS ONE experiment, mesquite was three to five times
    more abundant where prairie dogs were removed.
  - Resolution: the game says burrows help fresh grass grow and that bison favor the nutritious
    regrowth. It limits the plants that lose out to shrubs. It never says "more grass" or "fewer
    flowering plants" (Q09, Q10, Q13, Q14, Q20, and the Explorer O2 card, table and watch-out). It
    makes no claim about the total amount of grass on colonies.
  - Sources: NPS "Black-tailed Prairie Dogs" (Scotts Bluff), NPS "Pipsqueaks of the Prairie" (Badlands),
    PLOS ONE 2016 (Ponce-Guevara et al.).
