## Review flags and resolutions
- **L23-RF1 (pp. 296–298, 302, 304–306):** OCR is empty or garbled on the image-heavy pages, and p. 304
  has a printed typo. → All twelve pages were rendered, with 220 dpi crops of Figure 6A and of the Key
  Science Concept. Labels, plotted points, boundary shapes and the gallium panel come from the images.
- **L23-RF2 (pp. 296–297, 300, 303, 305–306):** the opener calls the white fog over the dry ice "smoky
  vapor", and several figures draw water vapor and carbon dioxide gas as white clouds. Both gases are
  invisible; the visible fog is tiny liquid water droplets. The book's own Figure 3 caption says this
  for water vapor. → The game always calls both gases invisible. Q23 tests what the fog is made of,
  and Q09 and the Explorer O1 watch-out cover visible "steam". (Sources: textbook p. 300; ChemEd X
  (Kuntzleman, 2016) for the droplet explanation; USGS "Sublimation and the Water Cycle" for the
  −78.5°C sublimation point only. USGS's looser wording, that the fog is "cold carbon dioxide gas and
  cold, humid air", is not used. NLM MeSH: carbon dioxide is colorless.)
- **L23-RF3 (pp. 301, 303):** the book says "all substances have a specific melting point", and
  Figure 4 gives water's states by temperature alone. Only pure crystalline solids melt at one sharp
  temperature, and every melting or boiling point depends on pressure. → The game says "a pure
  substance, at a stated pressure". Every state question gives the pressure. The Explorer O4 watch-out
  notes that wax and glass soften over a range. (Sources: OpenStax Chemistry 2e 10.5; textbook p. 303.)
- **L23-RF4 (pp. 302–304):** some numbers in sections 5–6 disagree or sit close to a boundary:
  - The text says 50 kPa, but Figure 5B shows 52 kPa.
  - Figure 5A shows 62 and 98 kPa, while the text gives about 101 kPa at sea level.
  - The mountain cabin is at 62 kPa in one paragraph and 80 kPa in the next.
  - The book calls the boiling point at 80 kPa "much lower", but it is about 93.5°C. The plotted
    point (95°C, 80 kPa) sits only about 1.5°C past the boiling line.

  → The book's conclusions still hold. The game uses round, sourced values and keeps every water
  state-diagram point at least 10°C from a boundary (Q16, Q19 and Q20). The closest are Q19's −10°C at
  60 kPa and 110°C at 200 kPa. Q17 asks for the boiling temperature itself, not a state. (Sources:
  NIST water saturation table, reopened 2026-10-17 with 70 kPa ≈ 89.9°C added for Explorer O4; NOAA
  JetStream.)
- **L23-RF5 (pp. 306–307, flagged in the brief): phase conditions.** Decision:
  - Kept the page's correct facts:
    - At 101 kPa water is liquid from 0°C to 100°C.
    - Gallium is solid or liquid across that range.
    - Carbon dioxide is solid below −78.5°C.
    - STP is 0°C and 100 kPa, which matches IUPAC's 1982 definition. Some references still use
      101.325 kPa.
  - Dropped the claim "Under STP conditions, water is a solid". STP's 0°C is water's melting
    boundary, where ice and liquid water coexist.
  - The Key Science Concept's "relatively narrow range" wording is vague; the game states the ranges
    instead.
  - No question asks water's state at STP or at any boundary point. Q27 and Q28 test what STP is for,
    and Q28 uses a clear carbon dioxide case.
  - Explorer O3 adds that liquid carbon dioxide needs more than about 518 kPa.

  (Sources: OpenStax Chemistry 2e 10.4 and 9.2; USGS "Sublimation and the Water Cycle"; NIST data for
  carbon dioxide.)
- **L23-RF6 (p. 298):** the book says an ice cube's molecules can neither spread apart nor move
  closer together. In fact solids expand slightly when warmed, because the particles vibrate harder
  and sit a little farther apart; the particles themselves do not grow. → The game says a solid's
  volume is "nearly fixed". Q08 tests thermal expansion, and the Explorer O1 watch-out names the
  point. (Source: OpenStax University Physics Volume 2, 1.3.)
- **L23-RF7 (p. 305):** gallium's melting point is printed as 29.78°C; the reference value is
  29.7646°C. → The game says "about 30°C" and never tests the decimals. (Source: RSC Periodic Table,
  gallium.)
- **L23-RF8 (pp. 299–300):** the hourglass-sand and racquetball analogies are models with limits.
  Sand grains are solids, and a real ball slows down, while gas particles in a container keep their
  average speed at a steady temperature. → No question depends on either analogy, and the Explorer O1
  watch-out names their limits. (Textbook pp. 299–300.)
