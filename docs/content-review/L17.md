## Review flags and resolutions
- **L17-RF1** (pp. 219, 221, 231)
  - Issue: a mineral is defined without "naturally occurring" (USGS also says "inorganic").
  - Resolution: the glossary says a mineral is naturally formed. Q02 tests the composition and atom-pattern parts, that most minerals are compounds, and (since round 2) that natural glass is not a mineral.
  - Round 2: the issue now also says "specific chemical composition" is simplified. Plagioclase runs continuously from a sodium-rich form to a calcium-rich form, and Q02's limitations say so.
  - Sources: USGS, rock versus mineral FAQ; University of Minnesota, Plagioclase Feldspar.
- **L17-RF2** (pp. 222–223)
  - Issue: the Figure 3A and 3B callouts say granite and pumice come from "light elements like silicon", and gabbro and basalt from "heavy elements like iron".
  - Round 2 corrected the argument. The old text said "silicon is not an especially light element", which is weak, since silicon (28) is half as heavy as iron (56). The new text says gabbro is also rich in magnesium, and magnesium (24.3) is lighter than silicon (28.1). It also names both figures; the renders of pp. 222 and 223 show the callouts in both.
  - Resolution: content contrasts silica-rich, light-colored granite with iron- and magnesium-rich, dark, denser gabbro. No question asks which elements are light or heavy.
  - Sources: NPS, Igneous Rocks; Royal Society of Chemistry, Magnesium and Silicon.
- **L17-RF3** (p. 223)
  - Issue: pumice is "silica, or glass", and the terms pyroclastic material and lava are defined loosely.
  - Resolution: content calls pumice frothy volcanic glass and describes pyroclastic material as fragments from explosive eruptions. Q08 and Q10 rely on the cooling-speed and gas-bubble ideas. (Corrected in reviser round 1: the earlier record said these ideas are ones "the textbook and the sources agree on", which is not true of the page's "no crystals at all" claim; that claim is now handled in L17-RF11.)
  - Sources: USGS Volcano Hazards Program Glossary; NPS, Igneous Rocks.
- **L17-RF4** (pp. 225, 231)
  - Issue: limestone is said to crystallize out as water evaporates, shell limestone is grouped as "chemical", and the text uses the term "dissolved sediment".
  - Resolution: halite is the evaporation example (Q14, now a desert lake fed by mineral-carrying streams). Limestone is described as built mostly from shells. No question bins limestone as chemical versus biologic. Since reviser round 1 the glossary defines sediment as loose solid pieces and covers minerals that crystallize out of water under sedimentary rock.
  - Sources: NPS, Sedimentary Rocks; University of Minnesota, Halite.
- **L17-RF5** (p. 224)
  - Issue: breccia versus sandstone is explained only by slope steepness.
  - Resolution: Q12 keys "settled close to where they broke off", which agrees with both the textbook and the source, and adds a limitation that shape is a clue, not proof. Q30 (added in round 1) tests the energy idea directly: a river slowing as it enters a calm lake drops its sand near the mouth.
  - Sources: University of Pittsburgh, Conglomerates and Breccias; National Geographic, Delta.
- **L17-RF6** (pp. 226–227, 231)
  - Issue: "deformation" and "recrystallization" are treated as separate kinds of metamorphism, and contact recrystallization is said to need "high pressure".
  - Resolution: content keeps the textbook's labels as a simplified model and says heat drives the change beside magma. Q16 tests only the distance pattern and no melting. Q17 matches the textbook's parent-and-product pairs.
  - Round 2 (schist versus gneiss): the p. 226 body text says deformed crystals "line up parallel to each other, in bands". Figure 5A is more precise: schist's crystals "line up parallel to each other", and gneiss's crystals "separate into parallel bands". Geologists draw the same line, between foliation and compositional banding. Content and questions now keep "bands" for gneiss, and the shared p. 226 evidence record quotes both the text and the figure.
  - Sources: NPS, Metamorphic Rocks; USGS, metamorphic rocks FAQ.
- **L17-RF7** (pp. 226–227)
  - Issue: the cookie-dough analogy can suggest that metamorphic rock softens or melts.
  - Resolution: the O3 watch-out names the analogy's limit. Q15 now asks whether a rock has to melt to become metamorphic (no) and says in its limitations that some rock partly melts at the most extreme temperatures.
  - Sources: USGS, metamorphic rocks FAQ.
- **L17-RF8** (pp. 228–229)
  - Issue: this is a guardrail check rather than an error. The textbook correctly says any rock type can become any other, but Figure 6 is easy to read as a single loop and gives no timescales.
  - Resolution: the seed Q19 and Q20 teach branching routes, and Q19's rationale now gives a millions-of-years timescale. The O4 watch-out says the diagram shows possibilities, not a timetable, and the diagram's alt text now says metamorphic rock can be changed into a different metamorphic rock.
  - Round 2: the O4 watch-out no longer says "Real changes take thousands to millions of years". That absolute contradicted the O2 card ("almost instantly"). It now says many changes take thousands to millions of years, while some, such as pumice cooling or a rockfall, happen in moments. Q21 now shows the model with its arrows unlabeled.
  - Sources: National Geographic, Rock Cycle.
- **L17-RF9** (p. 220)
  - Issue: the photo labels give rounded, dated ages for the three formations.
  - Resolution: no question tests these ages.
- **L17-RF10** (pp. 218, 219, 221, 223, 224, 226, 228, 229)

  - Resolution: every page was rendered. The diagram facts used in content were taken from the renders, including the four minerals of granite, the schist and gneiss conditions, and the missing metamorphic callout on the Key Science Concept spread.
- **L17-RF11** (p. 223), added in reviser round 1
  - Issue: the page says pumice "has no crystals at all", and the Figure 3B caption and pumice callout repeat it. Real pumice is mostly volcanic glass but often carries crystals (phenocrysts) that grew before the eruption; measured Mount Pinatubo pumice holds about 24–42 vol% crystals. The first draft repeated the absolute in key idea 3, the O2 card and table, the extrusive-rock glossary entry, and Q08–Q10.
  - Resolution: content says pumice is mostly glass because it cooled too fast for new crystals to grow, and the O2 card says any larger crystals grew before the eruption. Q08 compares how fast each final melt cooled (limitation stated). Q09 sorts pumice as extrusive because its melt froze as glass. Q10 asks about the glassy, bubbly part, with a limitation about earlier crystals.
  - Round 2:
    - The flag now says the 24–42 vol% was measured on a bubble-free (vesicle-free) basis.
    - Q10's prompt says "mostly glass", and its limitation says crystals can be "a large share" rather than "a few".
    - The USGS records in RF11, Q08 and Q10 now give the vesicle-free basis and the 58–76% glassy matrix.
  - Sources: USGS, Table 2 (Mount Pinatubo).
- **L17-RF12** (p. 222), added in reviser round 1
  - Issue: the page says igneous texture is determined by how fast magma cools. That is the main control, not the only one: water-rich magma grows the giant crystals of pegmatite without unusually slow cooling. The first draft of the O2 watch-out made it an absolute.
  - Resolution: the O2 watch-out now calls cooling speed the main control in this lesson's model and names the special case. Q07 holds composition constant, so its key is unaffected.
  - Sources: University of Pittsburgh, Pegmatitic Texture.
