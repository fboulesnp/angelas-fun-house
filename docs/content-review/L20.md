- L20-O1 (observe): identify the living and nonliving resources an organism needs, and recognize that different species need different mixes.

## Review flags and resolutions
- **L20-RF1** (pp. 263, 268–269)
  - Issue: the text calls temperature and rainfall "limited resources." Rainfall supplies water, a real resource, but temperature is a condition that organisms are affected by without using it up.
  - Resolution: content separates resources (water, light, nutrients, food) from conditions (temperature). No question asks learners to file temperature as a resource. Q02 sorts only true resources, Q20 frames grassland limits as rainfall, and Q21 uses warmth only as a condition.
  - Sources: University of Liège, Reflexions, "Ecological niche"; OpenStax Biology 2e 44.2; textbook pp. 268–269.
- **L20-RF2** (p. 267)
  - Issue: the grass graph labels kilograms per hectare as "population size." That unit measures mass of grass per area (biomass), not a count of plants.
  - Resolution: content uses "grass grown (kg per hectare)". Q15 teaches what the unit measures.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 46.2; textbook p. 267.
- **L20-RF3** (p. 267)
  - Issue: the graph page presents two correlations as cause and effect. The zebra caption mentions grass, but that graph plots rainfall. The two graphs cover different rainfall ranges (about 100–550 mm and 840–1,540 mm), so they cannot be chained numerically.
  - Resolution: Q17–Q19 separate pattern, explanation, prediction and assumption, and warn against predicting outside the measured range. Q14, Q16 and Q17 use original model data. No question combines numbers across the two textbook graphs. The model datasets carry different labels (Grassland A, B, C).
  - Sources: OpenStax Psychology 2e 2.2 (correlation is not causation); OpenStax Introductory Statistics 2e 12.5 (extrapolation is unreliable; added in round 1, opened 2026-10-17); textbook p. 267.
- **L20-RF4** (p. 266)
  - Issue: the text says yaks pull grass out by the roots while horses clip only the tops. Sources don't support this. Cattle wrap grass with the tongue and pull it against a pad on the upper jaw, staying a few centimeters above the soil (the Oregon State page describes cows, not "cattle-type grazers" in general). The yak review says yaks take tall grass with their tongues and use lips and incisors on short grass. Horses have incisors in both jaws and can graze to soil level.
  - Resolution: the resource-sharing idea is kept. Q09 and the O2 card describe yaks wrapping long grass with their tongues and horses biting with front teeth and cropping very short. Q09 now states its premise outright ("Suppose each style works best on grass of a different height"), because the feeding descriptions alone do not guarantee less overlap. Nothing says yaks uproot grass.
  - Sources: Oregon State University Forage Information System, "Regrowth: Animal habits, chewing" (paraphrase corrected in round 1); Shah et al. 2023, Frontiers in Veterinary Science (the yak review); textbook p. 266.
- **L20-RF5** (p. 268)
  - Issue: the Figure 4 caption says grasslands are usually at middle latitudes but lists savannas, which are tropical. The figure's own map shows tropical grassland.
  - Resolution: content places temperate grasslands (prairies, steppes, pampas) at middle latitudes and calls savannas tropical. Q21 (rewritten in round 1) asks learners to predict a savanna from warm, near-equator conditions with medium rain and a long dry season; its explanation repairs the "only middle latitudes" idea.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 44.3; textbook p. 268.
- **L20-RF6** (pp. 263–264)
  - Issue: the text says reproduction happens only within a species. That is too absolute: some different species produce hybrids, which are often infertile. Also, the textbook's own species example, the common dandelion, mostly sets seed without fertilization (apomixis).
  - Resolution: the glossary defines a species by offspring that can also reproduce, a definition that fits sexually reproducing organisms best. No question claims cross-species offspring are impossible, and no question uses dandelions to illustrate mating. Content follows the textbook in treating the common dandelion as one species.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 18.2; USDA Forest Service FEIS, *Taraxacum officinale* (added in round 1, opened 2026-10-17); textbook p. 264.
- **L20-RF7** (p. 270)
  - Issue: Figure 5's four levels skip the community level used in many references.
  - Resolution: Q25 and Q26 use only the textbook's four levels, so each has one defensible answer. Q26's extra choice is "Biome", not "Community". The O5 watchOut and the Q25 `limitations` field mention the community level.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 1.2; textbook p. 270.
- **L20-RF8** (p. 269)
  - Issue: needle-like boreal leaves are explained only as shedding snow. Other recognized advantages exist: needles stay on all year, warm quickly in spring and conserve nitrogen. Also, calling boreal trees simply "evergreen" (the p. 269 caption, and this lesson's first draft) is too absolute: larches are deciduous conifers, and larch dominates much of eastern Siberia's boreal forest.
  - Resolution: the glossary, the O4 card and table, and the Q22 rationale now say boreal forests are dominated by mostly evergreen, needle-leaved conifers (such as spruce, fir and pine). No reason for the needles is given, and no question tests it.
  - Sources: OpenStax Biology 2e 44.3; Schulze et al. 2012, Biogeosciences 9:1405–1421 (larch-dominated zone of eastern Siberia, at the boundary of evergreen and deciduous conifers; abstract page opened 2026-10-17); USDA Forest Service FEIS, *Larix laricina* (tamarack is a deciduous conifer of boreal Canada; opened 2026-10-17); textbook p. 269.
- **L20-RF9** (pp. 262, 263, 267, 268, 270)
  - Issue: OCR gaps and garbling.
  - Resolution: every page was read from its render, and the figures again at 250 dpi.
- **L20-RF10** (pp. 262–263, 271) — added in round 1
  - Issue: the Introduction calls the opener-photo horses wild horses that survive far from humans, but the opener photo shows a rider among the herd and the p. 271 photo shows a herder on horseback, captioned as caring for horses that came from wild populations. Mongolia's wild-type horse (Przewalski's horse, or takhi) was last seen in the wild in the 1960s and now lives only at reintroduction sites. The first-draft hook ("Wild horses thrive on Mongolia's windswept grasslands") was therefore unsupported.
  - Resolution: the hook now reads "Horses graze freely on Mongolia's windswept grasslands…". Content uses the textbook's "wild horses" only as model grazers and makes no claim about wild-horse numbers or status. No IUCN category is stated, because any status would be a dated figure.
  - Sources: Smithsonian's National Zoo and Conservation Biology Institute, "Przewalski's horse" (opened 2026-10-17); textbook pp. 262–263 and 271.
