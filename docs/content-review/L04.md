## Review flags and resolutions
- **L04-RF1** (pp. 42, 47, 48)
  - Issue: OCR is unusable on the opener and garbles the photosynthesis equation and the Key Science Concept labels. The p. 47 text also calls the equation a "chemical formula".
  - Resolution: read from the renders (Figure 2B also at 220 dpi). The equation is 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with 6 C, 12 H and 18 O on each side. Content says "chemical equation" for the whole reaction and "formula" for each substance.
  - Sources: textbook pp. 47–48 (rendered).
- **L04-RF2** (pp. 43, 46–48, 50, 53)
  - Issue: energy is described as "stored in chemical bonds", and cells "release the energy stored in the bonds". Taken literally, that contradicts the guardrail that breaking bonds requires energy.
  - Resolution: content says light energy ends up stored as chemical energy in sugar and is released when sugar reacts with oxygen. The O2 watchOut explains the shorthand, and no question asks where in a molecule energy sits. The rewritten Q12 keys "stored as chemical energy in the sugar" and "released when the sugar reacts with oxygen", never bond breaking.
  - Sources: RSC Education, "Chemical energetics: words matter". OpenStax Biology 2e 8.1 shows the shorthand is common. OpenStax Biology 2e 6.3 (energy changes form; some becomes heat) backs Q12.
- **L04-RF3** (p. 46)
  - Issue: the text says chlorophyll does not absorb green light. In fact, leaves absorb about 70–80 percent of green light (about 90 percent of red and blue), and absorbed green light drives photosynthesis.
  - Resolution: Q07 is keyed to "absorbs less green than red or blue". Q08 is now a one-hour test on two leaves and predicts that the green-lit leaf still makes some sugar; its explanation tells learners the textbook's wording is a simplification. The O1 watchOut covers it.
  - Source: The University of Tokyo, "Why are leaves green?" (Terashima).
- **L04-RF4** (pp. 49–50)
  - Issue: the Figure 3 labels can be read as "plants give off CO₂ only in the dark". Brief guardrail: plants photosynthesize and respire.
  - Resolution: Q15 teaches that sugar breakdown continues day and night and that photosynthesis outpaces it in bright light. Q18's limitation and Q20's "leaf cell at midnight" carry the same point. The O3 watchOut covers it.
  - Sources: Encyclopedia of the Environment (Dizengremel, Université de Lorraine); Botany online (University of Hamburg); textbook p. 50.
- **L04-RF5** (p. 49)
  - Issue: the textbook's "about 70 percent from carbon dioxide, most of the rest from water" for Saussure does not match modern dry-mass estimates. Those put about 90 percent (carbon plus oxygen) as tracing to CO₂, about 6 percent hydrogen from water, and about 4 percent soil minerals.
  - Resolution: the percentage is never tested. Q16 says "most" and defines dry mass; its rationales use the 4 and 6 percent figures.
  - Sources: Carbon TIME (Michigan State University, re-opened 2026-10-15); Botany online (University of Hamburg; also van Helmont's willow).
- **L04-RF6** (pp. 43, 51)
  - Issue: p. 51 says "all other organisms" depend on photosynthesis, while p. 43 says "nearly all". Chemosynthetic vent and seep communities are the exception.
  - Resolution: content says "nearly all", and the O4 watchOut now names vent and seep communities. Q23 uses a single grassland chain.
  - Source: NOAA Ocean Exploration, photosynthesis vs chemosynthesis (re-opened 2026-10-15; names hydrothermal vents and cold seeps).
- **L04-RF7** (p. 45)
  - Issue: the text says cyanobacteria live in "boiling-hot" springs. Their growth limit is about 70–74 °C.
  - Resolution: content does not repeat the hot-spring claim, and no question tests the temperature. (Round 1 corrected this resolution, which had described content that does not exist.)
  - Source: University of Edinburgh, The Microbial World (Deacon), Yellowstone thermophiles.
- **L04-RF8** (pp. 52–53)
  - Issue: the biofuel section is dated or simplified:
    - It calls jet biofuel "low-cost". As of 2026, IATA reports that it costs several times more than conventional jet fuel and expects it to be under 1 percent of jet fuel use (about 0.8 percent, about 2.4 Mt).
    - It says sugars become "alcohol that is used as fuel". In practice, alcohols are upgraded into jet-fuel hydrocarbons and blended with conventional fuel.
    - It uses "efficient" for energy per unit mass.
  - Resolution: questions test the criteria, constraints and trade-off reasoning, not market claims. Q26's limitation and the O5 card give the dated (as of 2026) figures, content uses "energy per kilogram", and Q27 repairs "biofuel releases no CO₂" (the textbook itself says it does).
  - Round 2: Q26's prompt now says "In the textbook's design story". Q26 key e and the O5 card keep the textbook's hedge ("might go to landfills"; "might otherwise go to a landfill"), because much city cardboard is recycled and many yard trimmings are composted.
  - Sources: IATA SAF page (re-opened 2026-10-15); U.S. DOE Alternative Fuels Data Center (SAF); U.S. DOE Alternative Fuels and Feedstocks Office (SAF).
- **L04-RF9** (p. 50)
  - Issue: cellulose "looks like many sugar molecules bound together". Each link leaves out the atoms of one water molecule (each unit is glucose minus H₂O).
  - Resolution: content says "linked sugar units". Round 2 changed the Q21 explanation, the O4 watchOut and this flag from "a water molecule is given off at each link" to "each link leaves out the atoms of one water molecule".
    - Reason (science review): water given off at each link is the classroom dehydration-synthesis model. The reviewer noted that plant cellulose synthase adds glucose from UDP-glucose and releases UDP.
    - Content makes no mechanism claim, so that point needs no source here. The composition wording is exact either way.
  - Source: OpenStax Biology 2e 3.2 Carbohydrates (glucose polymers; composition).
- **L04-RF10** (p. 50), new in round 1
  - Issue: the Figure 4 caption says cellulose "makes up the bulk of plant bodies". Read as "most of a plant", that overstates it: dry wood is about 38–49 percent cellulose.
  - Resolution: content calls cellulose the main material of plant cell walls and the largest single ingredient of wood, with cotton as the nearly pure example (glossary, Q21). Q20 says a cell wall is built mainly from cellulose. Evidence records quote the textbook's "the bulk of plant bodies" instead of "most". No question asks what share of a plant is cellulose.
  - Sources: USDA Forest Service, Forest Products Laboratory, "Cell wall chemistry" (Rowell, Pettersen et al. 2005); Frontiers in Plant Science, Haigler et al. 2012 (mature cotton fiber > 90 percent cellulose); OpenStax Biology 2e 3.2.
- **L04-RF11** (p. 49), new in round 2
  - Issue: Figure 3 labels "oxygen bubbles" on potted plants kept in air under glass jars, and the text says Ingenhousz collected and analyzed bubbles of gas. Visible gas bubbles form only on plants under water; in the rendered figure the "bubbles" are drawn molecule callouts. Ingenhousz's own bubble observations used leaves plunged in jars of water.
  - Resolution:
    - The O3 card's Ingenhousz paragraph now describes leaves in jars of water gathering bubbles in sunshine and hardly any after sunset. A new paragraph separates his observations from his conclusion. The table row and alt text match, and the watchOut says the drawn bubbles are symbols.
    - Q13 and Q14 keep the textbook's potted-plant setup but speak of oxygen given off, not bubbles. Q17 now states a water plant sealed in a jar of water.
  - Sources: textbook p. 49 Figure 3 (rendered); Le Moyne College, Classic Chemistry website, Ingenhousz, *Experiments upon Vegetables* (1779), excerpt (opened 2026-10-17).
    - The excerpt calls the gas "dephlogisticated air" and never says "oxygen".
    - The card's "turned out to be rich in oxygen" therefore rests on the textbook's p. 49 statement that the green parts produce oxygen in light.
- Not flagged:
  - The p. 45 statements that phytoplankton (including diatoms and cyanobacteria) photosynthesize near the sunlit surface are consistent with NASA Earth Observatory "What are Phytoplankton?" (opened). No external record was needed, because Q05 rests on the textbook page.
  - Historical dates (Ingenhousz late 1700s, Saussure early 1800s) match Botany online. They are used only in the O3 card, not tested.
